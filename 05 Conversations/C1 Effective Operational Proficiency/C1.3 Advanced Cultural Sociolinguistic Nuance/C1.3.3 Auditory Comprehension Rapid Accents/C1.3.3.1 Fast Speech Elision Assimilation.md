# Phonetic Reductions, Elision, and Assimilation in Fast Speech — Conversational Sentences

Each pair below shows the same exchange in careful pronunciation and in the fast,
informal-spelling version a learner might actually hear. Read the fast-speech line
aloud to train your ear to connect the sound to the full form.

## *-ado / -ada* Elision

The *d* between two vowels weakens and disappears in casual speech, most often in
past participles.

| Careful | Fast speech | English | Notes |
| ------- | ----------- | ------- | ----- |
| —¿Ya terminaste el trabajo? —Sí, ya está terminado. | —¿Ya terminaste el trabajo? —Sí, ya 'tá terminao. | "Did you finish the work already?" — "Yes, it's already finished." | Both *está* and *terminado* reduce in the same sentence — very typical of fast casual speech. |
| —¿Cómo llegaste tan rápido? —Estaba apurado. | —¿Cómo llegaste tan rápido? —Taba apurao. | "How did you get here so fast?" — "I was in a hurry." | *Estaba* loses its initial *es-* the same way *estoy/está* often do. |
| —¿Está fresco el pescado? —Sí, lo compré esta mañana. | —¿'Tá fresco el pescao? —Sí, lo compré esta mañana. | "Is the fish fresh?" — "Yes, I bought it this morning." | *Pescado* → *pescao* is one of the most common *-ado* reductions. |
| —¿Ya está lavada la ropa? —Todavía no, está mojada. | —¿Ya 'tá lavá la ropa? —Todavía no, 'tá mojá. | "Is the laundry washed already?" — "Not yet, it's wet." | Shows the *-ada* feminine ending reducing all the way to a single vowel in very fast speech, not just losing the *d*. |
| —Parece que estás enojado. —No, solo estoy cansado. | —Parece que 'tás enojao. —No, solo 'toy cansao. | "You seem upset." — "No, I'm just tired." | Both *estás* and *estoy* show the same initial *es-* dropping pattern. |

## *Para*, *Está*, and *Nada* Reduction

Three of the most frequent short-word reductions in casual conversation.

| Careful | Fast speech | English | Notes |
| ------- | ----------- | ------- | ----- |
| —¿Este regalo es para mí? —Sí, es para ti. | —¿Este regalo es pa' mí? —Sí, es pa' ti. | "Is this gift for me?" — "Yes, it's for you." | *Para* → *pa'* happens before almost any following word, not just pronouns. |
| —¿Está listo el café? —Está bien caliente. | —¿'Tá listo el café? —'Tá bien caliente. | "Is the coffee ready?" — "It's nice and hot." | *Está* reduces to *'tá* constantly in fast speech, almost like a separate short word. |
| —¿Tienes hambre? —No, no quiero nada. | —¿Tienes hambre? —No, no quiero na'. | "Are you hungry?" — "No, I don't want anything." | *Nada* → *na'* at the end of a sentence is extremely common and easy to miss if you're expecting the full word. |
| —¿Para qué es esto? —Para que no se dañe. | —¿Pa' qué es esto? —Pa' que no se dañe. | "What's this for?" — "So it doesn't get damaged." | Shows *para que* reducing as a fixed chunk, not word by word. |
| —No hay nada que hacer, está todo listo para la fiesta. | —No hay na' que hacer, 'tá to' listo pa' la fiesta. | "There's nothing to do, everything's ready for the party." | Stacks three reductions (*nada*, *está*, *todo*, *para*) in one sentence — realistic density for fast casual speech. |

## Nasal Assimilation and Vowel Linking

Sounds shifting to match their neighbors, and vowels across word boundaries
merging into one syllable.

| Careful | Fast speech | English | Notes |
| ------- | ----------- | ------- | ----- |
| —¿Me traes un vaso de agua? —Con un poco de hielo, ¿verdad? | —¿Me traes um vaso de agua? —Con um poco de hielo, ¿verdá? | "Can you bring me a glass of water?" — "With a little ice, right?" | *Un* before *vaso* and *poco* both shift toward *um*, since *v* and *p* trigger the same nasal assimilation as *b*. |
| —Mi amigo llega en un momento. —¿Va a traer a otra persona? | —Miamigo llega en un momento. —¿Va a traer a otra persona? | "My friend is arriving in a moment." — "Is he going to bring another person?" | *Mi amigo* links into one stretch of sound; *va a traer* shows the *a* + *a* also blending, without dropping the meaning. |
| —¿De ella es este cuaderno? —No, es de Andrea. | —¿Della es este cuaderno? —No, es de Andrea. | "Is this notebook hers?" — "No, it's Andrea's." | *De ella* is one of the clearest vowel-linking examples, since *e* and *e* merge almost completely in speech. |
| —Un beso y un abrazo antes de que te vayas. | —Um beso y un abrazo antes de que te vayas. | "A kiss and a hug before you go." | Only the *un* before *beso* assimilates (before *b*); the *un* before *abrazo* doesn't change, since a vowel follows instead of *b/p/v*. |
| —Voy a ver a mi abuela esta tarde. | —Voy a ver a miabuela esta tarde. | "I'm going to see my grandmother this afternoon." | *Mi abuela* links the same way *mi amigo* does — any vowel-final possessive before a vowel-initial noun tends to merge. |

## Regional Final *-S* Weakening

Common in Caribbean and coastal speech — much less common in Andean or highland
speech, so this pattern varies more by region than the others.

| Careful | Fast speech (Caribbean-style) | English | Notes |
| ------- | ------------------------------ | ------- | ----- |
| —¿Cómo están los chicos? —Están todos bien, gracias. | —¿Cómo 'tán lo(h) chico(h)? —'Tán to(h) bien, gracia(h). | "How are the kids?" — "They're all doing well, thanks." | Shows final *-s* aspirating (a light breathy sound) rather than disappearing completely — the more common outcome. |
| —¿Vamos a las tres o a las cuatro? —Mejor a las tres. | —¿Vamo(h) a la(h) tre(h) o a la(h) cuatro? —Mejor a la(h) tre(h). | "Are we going at three or four?" — "Better at three." | Demonstrates how heavily final *-s* weakening can stack across a whole sentence in fast Caribbean speech. |
| —Los amigos de mis primos viven en Puerto Rico. | —Lo(h) amigo(h) de mi(h) primo(h) viven en Puerto Rico. | "My cousins' friends live in Puerto Rico." | The plural markers weaken throughout, but the sentence stays understandable because word order and context carry the plural meaning. |

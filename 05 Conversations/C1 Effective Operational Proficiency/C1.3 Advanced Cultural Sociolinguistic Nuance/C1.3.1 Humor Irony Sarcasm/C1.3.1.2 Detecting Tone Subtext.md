# Detecting Tone and Subtext in Social Interactions — Conversational Sentences

## Sarcastic Agreement

Agreeing with someone's words while the flat or exaggerated tone signals the speaker
actually disagrees or is annoyed.

| Spanish | English | Notes |
| ------- | ------- | ----- |
| —Vamos a llegar tarde otra vez. —Sí, claro que sí, como siempre. | "We're going to be late again." — "Yeah, sure, like always." | *Claro que sí* paired with *como siempre* ("like always") signals resigned, sarcastic agreement rather than genuine enthusiasm — the speaker is frustrated, not agreeing happily. |
| —Dice que él nunca miente. —Ya, ya, lo que digas. | "He says he never lies." — "Sure, sure, whatever you say." | Repeating *ya* twice, plus *lo que digas* ("whatever you say"), pushes past simple acknowledgment into open disbelief — the speaker clearly doesn't buy it. |
| —El nuevo plan va a funcionar perfecto. —Obvio, no hay forma de que falle. | "The new plan is going to work perfectly." — "Obviously, there's no way it can fail." | Said with a flat or exaggerated tone, this reads as mocking the plan's chances rather than endorsing them — the literal words praise the plan, the delivery undercuts it. |
| —Mi hermano promete que esta vez sí va a pagar la renta a tiempo. —Ay, sí, cómo no. | "My brother promises he'll pay rent on time this time." — "Oh yeah, sure." | *Ay, sí* stacked with *cómo no* is a common two-part sarcastic-agreement combo — both pieces individually can be sincere, but together with flat delivery they signal "I don't believe that for a second." |
| —El gerente dice que valora mucho a su equipo. —Sí, se nota muchísimo. | "The manager says he values his team a lot." — "Yeah, it really shows." | *Se nota muchísimo* ("it really shows") is normally a compliment — used sarcastically here, it implies the opposite: it doesn't show at all. |

## Ironic Compliments

A compliment whose literal meaning is positive but whose tone or context flips it
into a criticism.

| Spanish | English | Notes |
| ------- | ------- | ----- |
| —Llegaste solo una hora tarde. Qué puntual eres. | "You only showed up an hour late. How punctual you are." | *Qué puntual eres* ("how punctual you are") is a textbook ironic compliment — praising punctuality right after pointing out lateness makes the sarcasm obvious even without hearing the tone. |
| —Rompiste el plato favorito de mamá. Buen trabajo. | "You broke Mom's favorite plate." — "Good job." | *Buen trabajo* ("good job") normally praises effort or skill; applied to an accident, it becomes a dry, sarcastic jab rather than genuine praise. |
| —Otra vez se te olvidaron las llaves. Qué buena memoria tienes. | "You forgot your keys again." — "What a great memory you have." | Praising memory right after describing forgetfulness is the same setup-punchline structure as the plate example — the compliment's content directly contradicts the situation it follows. |
| —Contestaste todas las preguntas del examen mal. Excelente trabajo. | "You got every question on the test wrong." — "Excellent work." | *Excelente trabajo* is maximally positive wording, which is exactly what makes it land as sharp sarcasm here — the bigger the gap between the words and the outcome, the more obviously ironic the compliment reads. |
| —Manejas increíble... para alguien que apenas sacó la licencia. | "You drive amazing... for someone who barely got their license." | The pause (written here as an ellipsis) before the qualifying phrase mirrors the vocal pause a speaker would use — the compliment starts sincere-sounding, then the second half reveals the real, less flattering point. |

## Hedged Criticism

Diplomatic, softened phrasing used to express real disapproval without stating it
bluntly.

| Spanish | English | Notes |
| ------- | ------- | ----- |
| —¿Qué te pareció mi ensayo? —Tiene potencial, pero le falta trabajo. | "What did you think of my essay?" — "It has potential, but it needs work." | *Tiene potencial* ("it has potential") softens what is really a negative assessment — a direct "no me gustó" would feel harsher, so the hedge does the same job more gently. |
| —¿Cómo estuvo la charla del nuevo director? —Estuvo... interesante. | "How was the new director's talk?" — "It was... interesting." | *Interesante* ("interesting") is one of the most common hedge words in Spanish (and English) for "I didn't like it, but I don't want to say so directly" — the pause before it often signals the hedge is coming. |
| —¿Te convence la propuesta? —No es exactamente lo que teníamos en mente. | "Are you convinced by the proposal?" — "It's not exactly what we had in mind." | *No es exactamente lo que teníamos en mente* avoids a flat "no" or "está mal," but the message — this proposal misses the mark — comes through clearly to a native listener. |
| —¿Qué opinas del nuevo compañero? —Todavía se está adaptando. | "What do you think of the new coworker?" — "He's still adjusting." | *Todavía se está adaptando* ("he's still adjusting") is a polite way to say someone isn't performing well yet, without directly criticizing them — common in workplace register. |
| —¿Te gustó cómo cocinó tu suegra? —Tiene un sazón muy particular. | "Did you like how your mother-in-law cooked?" — "She has a very particular seasoning style." | *Muy particular* ("very particular/distinctive") is a classic hedge — it avoids saying the food was bad while still signaling it wasn't to the speaker's taste. |
| —¿Qué tal la presentación de ventas? —Se notó que la prepararon rápido. | "How was the sales presentation?" — "You could tell it was prepared quickly." | Framing the criticism as an observation about the prep process (rather than "estuvo mal") is a common way to soften feedback while still making the underlying judgment unmistakable. |

## Expressing Disbelief

Short interjections and phrases used to signal "I don't buy that" — ranging from
light skepticism to open sarcasm.

| Spanish | English | Notes |
| ------- | ------- | ----- |
| —Dice que corrió el maratón sin entrenar nada. —No me digas. | "He says he ran the marathon without training at all." — "You don't say." | Depending on tone, *no me digas* can be genuine surprise or dry disbelief — here, given how implausible the claim is, it leans sarcastic. |
| —Juró que iba a dejar de fumar este año. —Ya veremos. | "He swore he'd quit smoking this year." — "We'll see." | *Ya veremos* ("we'll see") is a milder, more neutral-sounding way to express doubt — less sharp than outright sarcasm, but still signals the speaker isn't convinced. |
| —El vendedor asegura que este carro nunca ha tenido problemas. —Cómo no, y yo soy astronauta. | "The salesman swears this car has never had problems." — "Sure, and I'm an astronaut." | Adding an absurd comparison (*y yo soy astronauta*, "and I'm an astronaut") after *cómo no* is a common way to make sarcastic disbelief unmistakable, even in writing. |
| —Me prometió que iba a cambiar. —Eso ya lo he escuchado antes. | "He promised me he'd change." — "I've heard that one before." | *Eso ya lo he escuchado antes* ("I've heard that before") expresses skepticism without needing a sarcasm marker at all — the implication of disbelief comes from the content itself. |
| —Según ella, nunca llega tarde a ningún lado. —Sí, ajá. | "According to her, she's never late to anywhere." — "Yeah, uh-huh." | *Sí, ajá* is a very casual, almost dismissive way to voice disbelief — common in relaxed spoken register between people who know each other well. |

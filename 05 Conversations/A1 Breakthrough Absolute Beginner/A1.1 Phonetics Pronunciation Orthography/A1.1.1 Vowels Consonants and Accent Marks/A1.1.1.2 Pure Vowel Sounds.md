# Pure Vowel Sounds — Conversational Sentences

Natural sentences a Spanish speaker would actually say, chosen to give practice
saying each pure vowel sound clearly and without an English-style glide.

## Everyday greetings and replies

Short, high-frequency phrases where every vowel must stay pure — easy to
rush and glide if you're not careful.

| Spanish        | English          | Notes                                              |
| -------------- | ---------------- | --------------------------------------------------- |
| Hola.          | Hi.              | Both *o* and *a* stay short and pure — no glide.     |
| Buenos días.   | Good morning.    | *o* in *buenos* and *í* in *días* both stay crisp.   |
| ¿Cómo estás?   | How are you?     | Watch the *o* in *cómo* — no "oh-oo" glide.          |
| Muy bien, gracias. | Very well, thanks. | *ia* in *gracias* is two pure vowels back to back. |
| Nos vemos.     | See you.         | *e* in *vemos* stays a clean "eh," not "ey."         |

## Simple statements about everyday things

Common nouns and short sentences built around single-syllable or clear
two-syllable words, one per vowel.

| Spanish        | English              | Notes                                            |
| -------------- | -------------------- | -------------------------------------------------- |
| Tengo una casa. | I have a house.     | *a* appears three times — keep every one identical. |
| Es un libro.    | It's a book.        | *i* in *libro* stays short and tight.               |
| El perro está aquí. | The dog is here. | *e* and *á* both stay pure, no reduction.          |
| La mesa es azul. | The table is blue. | *u* in *azul* is fully rounded, not drawn out.      |
| Tengo sed.      | I'm thirsty.         | *e* stays crisp even in a one-syllable word.        |

## Short questions and answers

Yes/no exchanges are common in real conversation and put the pure vowels
*sí* and *no* right next to each other, where the contrast matters most.

| Spanish              | English              | Notes                                       |
| -------------------- | -------------------- | --------------------------------------------- |
| ¿Tienes un libro?    | Do you have a book?  | *ie* in *tienes* is two pure vowels, not one blended sound. |
| Sí, tengo uno.        | Yes, I have one.     | *sí* is one short "ee," not drawn out.        |
| ¿Está aquí el perro?  | Is the dog here?     | *í* in *aquí* stays sharp and short.          |
| No, no está aquí.     | No, it's not here.   | Both *no* stay pure "oh" — no glide.          |
| ¿Es azul o rojo?      | Is it blue or red?   | *o* meaning "or" is one pure sound, distinct from *rojo*'s *o*. |

## Reactions and small talk

Short reactive phrases people say constantly — useful because their vowels
get swallowed fast in casual English speech, but must stay clear in Spanish.

| Spanish         | English           | Notes                                           |
| --------------- | ----------------- | -------------------------------------------------- |
| ¡Qué bueno!     | That's great!      | *ue* is two pure vowels said quickly together.     |
| Claro que sí.   | Of course.         | *a* in *claro* and *í* in *sí* both stay full-value. |
| No sé.          | I don't know.      | *é* in *sé* is a clean, short "eh."                |
| Gracias, igual. | Thanks, likewise.  | *ua* in *igual* — two pure vowels, no blending.    |
| Está bien.      | That's fine.       | *á* and *ie* both stay short and clear.            |

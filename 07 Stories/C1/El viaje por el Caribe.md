## English Translation

The Reyes family had been planning the trip for months: two weeks traveling through different islands and coastal cities in the Caribbean. Valentina, the oldest daughter, had studied Spanish for years and felt confident in her level, but from the very first day in Santo Domingo she realized that reality was different from what she had practiced in the classroom.

At the market, a vendor spoke to her so fast that Valentina could barely make out the words: "What do you want, sweetie? I've got mango, I've got avocado, all real fresh." Confused, Valentina asked him to repeat it more slowly. The vendor smiled and slowed down, though without losing that characteristic singsong tone of Dominican Spanish, where the S sounds nearly disappear at the end of words.

Days later, in Cartagena, the family met a tour guide who spoke with a completely different cadence, more clipped and rhythmic, typical of Colombia's Caribbean coast. "Ah, my dear, here in the walled city we've got plenty of history," he told them, stretching out the vowels in a way Valentina found musical. Her younger brother, Mateo, who had only been studying Spanish for a year, understood almost nothing, and Valentina had to quietly translate some phrases for him.

In Puerto Rico, the family ran into an even faster pace of speech, full of words Valentina didn't recognize from her classes: "Hey, what's up? Let's go to the beach, brother, it's amazing." Valentina noticed that many Puerto Ricans naturally switched between Spanish and English within the same sentence, something her lessons had never mentioned.

Toward the end of the trip, in Havana, an elderly man selling coffee on the street explained to her, patiently, that every Caribbean country had its own music when it spoke. "We Cubans eat our letters," he told her, laughing, "but if you pay attention to the rhythm, you'll understand more than you think." Valentina began noticing patterns: the dropping of certain consonants, the constant use of diminutives, and expressions that changed from island to island, even though the language remained, in essence, the same.

Back home, Valentina realized her ear had been trained in a way no textbook could have achieved. She no longer depended on hearing every word with perfect clarity, but had learned to grasp the overall meaning from rhythm, context, and intonation. "The Spanish I learned in class," she told her mother, "is only the starting point. The real language lives in how each person speaks it."

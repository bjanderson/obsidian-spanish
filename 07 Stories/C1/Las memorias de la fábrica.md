## English Translation

Don Alberto was eighty-two years old and, sitting on his porch, he loved telling his grandchildren stories about the forty years he had worked at the town's textile factory. "I started there at eighteen," he'd tell them, "sweeping the floors, with no idea that one day I'd become supervisor of the whole plant." His grandchildren, though they'd heard the story many times before, always came back asking him to tell it again.

Once he finished high school, Alberto had no money to keep studying, so he looked for work right away. "Having lost my father that same year, I knew I had to help out at home," he'd explain. The factory, which made cotton fabric for the whole region, hired young people with no experience and trained them from scratch. Alberto started out cleaning, but, watching every machine closely, he quickly learned how the whole process worked.

Having shown interest and discipline, within two years he was promoted to operator of one of the weaving machines. "They were loud, dangerous machines," he recalled, showing his grandchildren a scar on his left hand. "One moment of carelessness, and you could lose a finger. Working there, I learned to respect every machine like it was an animal that could bite." Once his shift ended, many nights he'd get home exhausted, smelling of oil and cotton, but proud of having done his job well.

Over the years, and having earned his bosses' trust, Alberto kept moving up: first to line supervisor, then to shift supervisor, and finally to general supervisor of the whole plant. "I got to know every worker by name," he'd say proudly. "Knowing how to listen to people's problems, you get the work to run better than by just giving orders." His grandchildren, listening to him, understood that job had been much more than just a job to their grandfather.

The factory closed fifteen years ago, a victim of foreign competition and more modern machinery in other countries. "Once production ended for the last time, I walked through the empty plant and felt a chapter of my life closing," Alberto recalled, his voice a little shaky. Many of his old coworkers, retired like him, still got together once a month for coffee and to remember old times.

When he finished telling the story, one of his grandchildren asked him if he missed working. "I miss the noise of the machines and the people," Alberto admitted, smiling with nostalgia, "but most of all I miss feeling like, every day, I was building something with my own hands." Having devoted his whole life to that place, Alberto knew that, even though the factory no longer existed, the lessons he'd learned there still remained part of who he was.

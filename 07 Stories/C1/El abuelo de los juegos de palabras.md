## English Translation

Don Emilio was seventy years old and had a well-earned reputation in the family: no one could have a normal conversation with him without ending up caught in some kind of wordplay. His grandchildren had learned, from a young age, that any innocent sentence could become the perfect excuse for a bad joke. "Grandpa, can you pass the salt?" someone would ask at the table, and Emilio would answer without fail: "The salt? I'd rather stay, thanks."

His oldest granddaughter, Camila, who was studying linguistics in college, was the only one who genuinely appreciated the cleverness behind those jokes, though she also pretended to complain like everyone else. "Grandpa, your jokes are terrible," she'd tell him, smiling. "I know," he'd reply, proudly, "but they're terribly good, which is different." And then he'd let out a laugh that spread to the whole table, no matter how many times they'd heard the same joke before.

One Sunday, while the family was playing guessing games after lunch, Emilio proposed his specialty: double meanings. "What does a bee do at the gym?" he asked, looking at his youngest grandchildren with a mischievous smile. No one knew the answer. "Zumba," he finally said, "because it never stops buzzing." (The joke plays on *zumbar*, "to buzz," and *Zumba*, the dance-fitness class — a real double meaning in Spanish.) The kids laughed without fully understanding the joke, while the adults groaned at how bad the pun was.

Camila, inspired, decided to try one herself. "Grandpa, do you know how to say handkerchief in Japanese?" Emilio looked at her with genuine interest, surprised his granddaughter had decided to play along. "No, tell me," he replied, expectant. "Sacamoco" (a nonsense word that sounds vaguely Japanese but literally means "nose-picker" in Spanish), Camila said, holding back laughter. Emilio let out such a loud laugh that he nearly choked on his food, proud that his granddaughter had, after all, inherited a taste for a bad joke.

That night, while helping her grandfather wash the dishes, Camila asked him why he liked wordplay so much. Emilio thought for a moment before answering. "When I was young, my father used to make the same kind of bad jokes, and I found them unbearable," he admitted. "But now that he's gone, every time I make a joke like that, I feel like he's still a little bit present. Humor, sometimes, is a way of remembering the people we love."

Camila, moved, understood then that her grandfather's jokes weren't just a harmless habit, but a quiet way of keeping a family tradition alive, word by word, generation after generation. From that night on, she started writing down Emilio's best jokes in a notebook, with the idea that, someday, she'd tell them herself to her own grandchildren.

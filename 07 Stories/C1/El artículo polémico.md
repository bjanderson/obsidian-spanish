## English Translation

During breakfast, Ricardo read a headline aloud that struck him as alarming: "The city faces an unprecedented crisis in its public transportation system." He passed the newspaper to his wife, Camila, convinced the situation was as serious as the headline suggested. Camila, however, read the whole article before offering an opinion.

"Look at how this is written," Camila told him, pointing to the second paragraph. "The headline says 'unprecedented crisis,' but the article itself only mentions a fifteen percent increase in delays over the past month. That's a problem, no doubt, but it's not the same as a crisis." Ricardo, a little surprised, reread the article more carefully and noticed Camila was right: the headline's language considerably exaggerated what the data actually showed.

"The media does this all the time," Camila went on. "They use dramatic language to grab attention, even when the actual content is more nuanced. Notice whose byline is on the article, too: it's an opinion column, not an objective news piece. The author clearly holds a political position against the current city administration." Ricardo checked the top of the article and, sure enough, found the label "Opinion" in small letters, something he hadn't noticed before.

That afternoon, Ricardo decided to look for other sources on the same topic to compare the coverage. He found an article in a different newspaper that presented the same data but with a much more neutral tone, also quoting a city spokesperson who explained the causes behind the increase in delays, among them maintenance work planned well in advance.

That night, over dinner, Ricardo told Camila what he had discovered. "You were right," he admitted. "The first article wasn't exactly lying, but it chose what information to highlight and what to leave out to create a specific impression. I think I need to be more critical of what I read, instead of just reacting to the headline." Camila smiled, pleased that the conversation had sparked a genuine interest in her husband to read more carefully.

From then on, Ricardo made a habit of reading several sources before forming an opinion on any controversial topic, and of always distinguishing between an objective news piece and an opinion column. "It's not about distrusting everything I read," he explained to a friend weeks later, "but about understanding that every outlet has its own way of telling the same story."

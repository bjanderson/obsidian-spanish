## English Translation

Andrea worked in the analytics department of a logistics company, and every quarter it fell to her to draft the performance report presented to the board of directors. Although she had been doing this job for two years, she still found it hard to strike the balance between conveying information accurately and keeping a tone objective enough for the report to come across as professional.

Her first draft tended to sound too personal: "I think sales dropped because we had several problems with suppliers." Her boss, Fernando, pointed out that that kind of language wasn't appropriate for a formal document. "In a report," he explained, "you avoid using the first person. Instead of saying 'I think,' you present the data and let it speak for itself. For example, you could write: 'a decline in sales is observed, attributable mainly to delays in the supply chain.'"

Andrea took note of the advice and began reviewing the rest of the document with that same logic in mind. She replaced phrases like "I think we should improve" with more impersonal constructions: "it is recommended that improvements be implemented in the supplier selection process." She also learned to use academic connectors to better structure her arguments: "first of all," "likewise," "however," and "consequently," which helped the text flow more logically and professionally.

While reviewing the conclusions section, Fernando noticed that Andrea was still using overly categorical language. "Avoid absolute statements," he suggested, "unless the data fully supports them. Instead of saying 'this guarantees next quarter's success,' it's more accurate to say 'this could contribute to better results next quarter.'" Andrea understood that the goal wasn't to soften the truth, but to accurately reflect the level of certainty the data actually allowed.

After several rounds of revisions, Andrea presented the final report to the board of directors. The document, written in a clear, impersonal register backed by concrete data, was met with positive feedback. One of the directors even praised the clarity with which complex trends had been presented, without falling into generalizations or an unnecessarily alarmist tone.

That afternoon, Andrea reflected on what she had learned during the process. Writing objectively, she realized, didn't mean eliminating critical analysis, but presenting it so that the data, not personal opinions, formed the basis of each conclusion. She saved a copy of the report as a reference for next quarter, knowing each version would help her further refine her professional style.

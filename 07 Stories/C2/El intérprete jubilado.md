## English Translation

Don Salvador Iturriaga had been retired for fifteen years, but when his granddaughter Camila asked him to tell her about his work as an interpreter at the United Nations, his eyes lit up as if he had suddenly turned thirty again.

"Simultaneous interpreting," he began, pouring himself a coffee while Camila settled in across from him with her notebook for college, "doesn't consist of translating word for word. It consists of listening to a complete idea while you're speaking the previous one, and trusting that your brain will find the right words before the sentence ends. It's like walking a tightrope while describing the scenery."

Camila, fascinated, asked him if he had ever made a mistake in front of an international audience. Don Salvador let out a laugh that shook his shoulders. "More than once," he admitted. "I remember one time when a Russian delegate used an expression that, translated literally, made no sense at all in Spanish: something like 'hanging noodles on someone's ears.' I had exactly two seconds to decide what to do, so I reformulated the idea in real time and said 'pulling someone's leg,' which conveyed the same idea of deceiving with words in Spanish."

"And what if you had gotten it wrong?" Camila asked, with the curiosity of someone who hasn't yet made any serious professional mistakes. "You get it wrong," her grandfather replied, shrugging, "and you keep interpreting, because behind you there are a hundred people waiting for the next sentence. There's no time to dwell on it. What you learn, over the years, is to trust your instinct as much as your vocabulary."

He also told her about the time two heads of state nearly caused a diplomatic incident over an idiom mistranslated by another colleague, and about how he himself had once spent entire weeks studying the specific vocabulary of fishing agreements for a single conference that lasted barely three days. "People think the job ends when you walk out of the booth," he said, "but really it starts long before, when you prepare yourself to understand not just the words, but the way each delegate thinks."

Camila closed her notebook, realizing she had spent an hour listening without writing down a single word. "Grandpa, this is exactly what I want to do," she said, with a conviction that reminded Salvador of his own, decades earlier. He smiled, and for the first time in a long while, he felt that his career, with all its seconds of panic and its small invisible triumphs, hadn't fully ended: it was still alive, now, in his granddaughter's eyes.

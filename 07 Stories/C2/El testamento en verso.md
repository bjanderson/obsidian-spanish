# El testamento en verso

La abogada Renata Solórzano [[llevar|llevaba]] [[dos]] [[día|días]] enteros intentando descifrar el testamento de don Aurelio Contreras, un excéntrico coleccionista de [[libro|libros]] que [[haber|había]] [[decidir|decidido]], para sorpresa de [[todo-todos|todos]], redactar su última voluntad en verso. "[[quien|Quien]] esto leyere y no comprendiere," [[decir|decía]] la [[primero-primera-primeros-primeras|primera]] estrofa, "que acuda a [[quien]] de leyes entendiere, pues no en prosa vulgar, [[sino]] en rima, he [[querer|querido]] [[dejar]] mi despedida." Renata suspiró: aquello no [[solo]] [[estar|estaba]] en un español arcaico, [[sino]] que además rimaba.

El resto del documento no [[ser|era]] [[más]] sencillo. "Si [[mi-mis|mis]] herederos disputaren la [[casa]] de la calle Alcázar," continuaba el testamento, "repártanla [[entre]] [[quien|quienes]] la habitaren, y si [[nadie|ninguno]] la quisiere, véndase, y el dinero que se obtuviere repártase por igual [[entre]] los que aún vivieren." Renata reconoció, con [[cierto-cierta-ciertos-ciertas|cierto]] alivio académico, el futuro de subjuntivo: "disputaren," "habitaren," "quisiere," "obtuviere," "vivieren." Formas que apenas se enseñaban [[ya]] en las facultades de derecho, [[pero]] que aún aparecían, como fósiles, en [[cierto-cierta-ciertos-ciertas|ciertos]] documentos legales y en el Código Civil.

Los tres [[hijo-hija|hijos]] de don Aurelio, reunidos en la oficina de Renata, escuchaban la lectura con expresiones que [[ir|iban]] de la confusión a la exasperación. "¿[[poder|Puede]] alguien explicarnos esto en español [[normal]]?" [[preguntar|preguntó]] el [[mayor]], cruzando los [[brazo|brazos]]. Renata [[sonreír|sonrió]], comprensiva. "Su [[padre-madre|padre]], con esta forma verbal, básicamente [[decir|dice]]: 'si en el [[futuro]] ustedes no se ponen de acuerdo sobre la [[casa]], [[entonces]] se vende y se reparte el dinero [[entre]] [[quien|quienes]] sigan con [[vida]].' La [[hijo-hija|hija]] menor, que hasta entonces [[haber|había]] permanecido en silencio, soltó una risa suave. "Típico de papá," [[decir|dijo]]. "Ni siquiera en su testamento [[poder|pudo]] [[ser]] directo. [[tener|Tuvo]] que convertir hasta la herencia en un acertijo." Los [[hermano-hermana|hermanos]], contagiados por su comentario, terminaron riéndose también, y la tensión que [[haber|había]] llenado la sala [[durante]] la lectura se disolvió, al [[menos]] por un [[momento]].

[[sonreír|Sonrió]], [[pensar|pensando]] que, arcaico o no, don Aurelio [[haber|había]] logrado exactamente [[lo|lo]] que quería: que [[nadie]], ni siquiera sus [[propio-propia-propios-propias|propios]] [[hijo-hija|hijos]] peleando por una [[casa]], pudiera olvidarlo del [[todo-todos|todo]].

## English Translation

Lawyer Renata Solórzano had spent two full days trying to decipher the will of don Aurelio Contreras, an eccentric book collector who had decided, to everyone's surprise, to write his last will and testament in verse. "Whoever should read this and fail to understand it," the first stanza read, "let them seek out one versed in law, for not in vulgar prose, but in rhyme, have I chosen to leave my farewell." Renata sighed: not only was this in archaic Spanish, it also rhymed.

The rest of the document was no simpler. "Should my heirs dispute the house on Alcázar Street," the will continued, "let it be divided among whoever should inhabit it, and should none of them want it, let it be sold, and whatever money is obtained shall be divided equally among those still living." Renata recognized, with a certain academic relief, the future subjunctive: "disputaren," "habitaren," "quisiere," "obtuviere," "vivieren." Forms barely taught anymore in law schools, but which still appeared, like fossils, in certain legal documents and in the Civil Code.

Don Aurelio's three children, gathered in Renata's office, listened to the reading with expressions ranging from confusion to exasperation. "Can someone explain this to us in normal Spanish?" asked the eldest, crossing his arms. Renata smiled, understanding. "Your father, with this verb form, is basically saying: 'if in the future you all don't agree on the house, then it gets sold and the money is divided among whoever is still alive.' The future subjunctive describes a hypothetical condition that may or may not come to pass, something that 'should happen to occur,' so to speak."

The youngest daughter, who had stayed silent until then, let out a soft laugh. "Typical of Dad," she said. "He couldn't even be direct in his own will. He had to turn even the inheritance into a riddle." Her siblings, caught up by her comment, ended up laughing too, and the tension that had filled the room during the reading dissolved, at least for a moment.

At the end of the meeting, as the siblings said their goodbyes, Renata silently reread the will's last verse: "And should some day someone forget me, may this paper, with its rhyme, prevent it." She smiled, thinking that, archaic or not, don Aurelio had achieved exactly what he wanted: that no one, not even his own children arguing over a house, could ever fully forget him.

## English Translation

Renata had just graduated in translation and interpreting, and her first job was as a support interpreter at an international business conference. Nervous but prepared, she had spent days reviewing the technical vocabulary she would probably need, never imagining that the real challenge wouldn't come from technical vocabulary, but from a simple colloquial expression.

Everything was going fine until one of the Mexican speakers, in the middle of an explanation about difficult negotiations, said casually: "We could already see that coming a while back." Renata froze for an instant. She knew exactly what the phrase meant — that the problem had been foreseeable, that no one should have been surprised — but she couldn't find any expression in English that captured that same tone, that mix of resignation and folk wisdom. She ended up translating, almost in a panic, "that had already been coming for a while," a grammatically correct sentence that sounded flat, without the cultural nuance of the original.

During the break, she went up to Marisol, an interpreter with twenty years of experience, and confessed her frustration. "I felt like I betrayed the meaning," Renata admitted. "I said the right words, but I didn't say what the speaker actually meant." Marisol smiled, like someone who has heard that same complaint hundreds of times. "That happens to everyone at first," she explained. "Literal translation almost never works with idioms, because an idiom doesn't translate words, it translates a way of seeing the world. What you have to look for isn't the exact equivalent, but the equivalent effect."

Marisol gave her an example: instead of translating literally, she could have chosen an idiomatic expression native to English, like "we saw the writing on the wall," which conveys the same idea of anticipating the inevitable, even though the words don't match at all. "The English-speaking listener doesn't need to know that in Spanish you say 'see it coming,'" Marisol added. "What they need is to feel the same thing the Spanish-speaking audience felt."

In the second half of the conference, Renata got another chance. When a Chilean speaker remarked, laughing, that a certain competitor "had been all talk," Renata, without hesitating this time, translated: "he was all talk and no action." It wasn't a literal translation, but the English-speaking audience laughed at exactly the same moment the Spanish-speaking audience had. Renata knew, in that instant, that she had stopped translating words and started translating meaning.

At the end of the day, as she packed up her notes, she realized that small triumph was worth more than any technical term she could have memorized. Being a translator, she finally understood, didn't mean knowing two languages, but inhabiting two ways of thinking at the same time.

## English Translation

Don Higinio was eighty-two years old and, according to his whole family, had a memory more organized than any dictionary. He had spent a good part of his youth traveling around the continent as a farm-machinery salesman, and in every town, in every market, he had gone about storing in his head the sayings and expressions people used to say, with humor or with wisdom, what ordinary words couldn't quite express.

Every Sunday, his three grandchildren came to eat at his house, and every Sunday, without fail, don Higinio brought out some new expression. "Do you know what 'estar en el quinto pino' means?" he asked that afternoon, while serving dessert. The grandchildren shook their heads. "It means being very, very far away," he explained, "although, truth be told, I learned that one from a Spanish colleague, it's not from around here. Let me teach you one of ours instead: in Venezuela they say 'estar arrecho,' which depending on the context can mean being furious or being great at something, so you have to be careful with the tone." The grandchildren laughed, and the eldest, Tomás, took out his phone to jot down the expression before he forgot it.

"Grandpa, why do you know so many?" the youngest, Antonella, asked him, with genuine curiosity. Don Higinio set his spoon down on the plate and considered his answer with more care than he usually gave his jokes. "Because every saying I learned, I learned from someone," he answered. "From a mechanic in Paraguay, from a tamale seller in Oaxaca, from a fisherman in Valparaíso. When I repeat one of those sayings, I'm not just saying words: I'm bringing back, for a second, the person who taught it to me."

The table fell silent for a moment, and Tomás, who was already seventeen and was starting to understand certain things differently, realized that his grandfather wasn't just collecting sayings: he was collecting the people who had given them to him, and now, with every passing Sunday, he was trying to give them to his grandchildren before it became too late to do so.

That night, back at his own house, Tomás opened a new notebook and wrote on the first page: "Sayings from my grandfather." Underneath, he jotted down the three he had learned that afternoon, and decided that, every Sunday, he would add one more, not just so he wouldn't forget them, but so that, someday, he too could bring back, with a single phrase, the person who had taught them to him.

## English Translation

Camila arrived in Buenos Aires with her suitcase full of clothes and her head full of nerves. She had studied Spanish her whole life, but she'd barely gotten off the plane when she noticed that Argentine Spanish sounded like another language: the rhythm, the intonation, even the way of conjugating verbs was different. At school, her new classmates greeted her with a "What's up, che?" that she could barely understand, and every time she opened her mouth to answer, she felt her Colombian accent give her away instantly.

Determined not to stand out because of her accent, Camila started practicing in secret every night, in front of the mirror in her room at her host family's house. She repeated phrases she had heard during the day, adjusting her intonation, swapping "tú" for "vos," and memorizing expressions like "posta," "quilombo," and "laburo." She recorded her own voice on her phone and compared it to her classmates', correcting every detail until, weeks later, no one at school could have guessed, just from the way she spoke, that she hadn't been born in that city.

The trouble started when her history teacher asked her to present a project on Argentine independence in front of the whole class. Camila, by then so used to talking with her friends in the relaxed tone of the schoolyard, began her presentation by saying: "So, che, like, it went like this, you know?" Her teacher, taken aback, cut her off with a stern look. "Camila, this is a formal presentation, not a chat between friends. I'd ask you to use a more appropriate register." Several classmates held back their laughter, and Camila felt her cheeks burning.

That afternoon, while walking home with her friend Sol, still embarrassed, she confessed how she felt. "I tried so hard to talk like you all that I forgot there are moments when even you don't talk that way," she admitted. Sol laughed, but kindly. "Che, it's not about always talking the same way," she explained, "it's about knowing when. With us you can talk however you want, but with the teacher, with your host parents, in an interview... there you need a different register, beyond whatever accent you use."

Camila finally understood that perfectly imitating an accent wasn't enough if she didn't also learn to move between the different levels of formality each situation demanded. From then on, she kept talking to her friends with the same enthusiasm as always, "vos" included, but she learned to shift register around adults, without losing either her Colombian accent or the new Argentine way of speaking she had worked so hard to master.

## English Translation

Mariana had gone three nights without sleeping well, trying to finish the poem she would submit to the school's literary contest. It wasn't enough for her that the words rhymed; she wanted every line to have a rhythm that would force the reader to pause, to reread, to feel the weight of every word exactly where she had placed it. "From the sea my words were born, not from me," she finally wrote, pleased with the inversion she had achieved: by placing "from the sea" first, instead of simply saying "my words were born from the sea," the line gained a force that her Literature teacher would surely recognize as hyperbaton.

The next morning, Mariana turned in the poem unsigned, as the contest rules required, and left it in the library's dropbox. Days later, when the finalist entries were published in the school newspaper, Emiliano, a classmate from her history class, read the whole poem without knowing who had written it. When he reached the last line, "Yours are my silences, though you never ask for them," he stopped, rereading it several times. Convinced he recognized Mariana's style from certain words she used to use in class, and noticing that the poem was dedicated "to the one who taught me to listen to the sea," he concluded, embarrassed and flattered at once, that those lines were about him.

That afternoon, in the hallway, Emiliano approached Mariana with an awkwardness that wasn't like him. "I read your poem," he told her, lowering his voice, "and I wanted to tell you that I also..." Mariana, confused, cut him off: "What poem? I haven't shown mine to anyone." Emiliano, growing more uncomfortable by the moment, insisted the style gave it away, and that the dedication could only refer to the afternoons they had spent together at the beach, studying for exams.

The next day, teacher Ibáñez decided to analyze the winning poem in front of the whole class, without yet revealing the author's name. "Look at this line," she said, pointing to the board where she had copied the first line, "'From the sea my words were born, not from me.' Here, the hyperbaton doesn't just beautify the line, it shifts the emphasis: the first thing the reader perceives isn't who is speaking, but where what they say comes from. And the dedication, 'to the one who taught me to listen to the sea,' doesn't refer to a particular person, but to the sea itself, which taught the author to listen to her own voice." Emiliano, sunk in his chair, felt his face burning while several classmates laughed discreetly, having already guessed whom the confusion was about.

When class ended, Mariana went up to Emiliano, with a smile that didn't hide how amused she was. "So you thought the poem was for you," she said, unable to hold back her laughter. He, still red-faced, admitted the word order had completely fooled him. "That's exactly what hyperbaton does," she replied, "it changes what one thinks they hear, even though the meaning, properly read, is something else." From then on, whenever someone in class wanted to exaggerate something, they'd say they were using "hyperbaton, Emiliano style."

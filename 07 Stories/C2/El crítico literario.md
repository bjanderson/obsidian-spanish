## English Translation

Literary critic Aurelio Fuentes had spent all morning rereading the same paragraph of "The Gardens of Silence," the novel he had to review before Friday. It wasn't the plot that had him stuck, but a single sentence, the first of the third chapter, which he had reread at least ten times: "From ancient memory, vague shadows emerged, while time, indifferent, kept to its eternal course."

Any hurried reader would have skimmed past that sentence without stopping, but Aurelio knew that there, in that seemingly arbitrary order of the words, lay much of the author's genius. In conventional prose, the sentence would have simply said: "Vague shadows emerged from ancient memory." But the author had chosen to invert the order, placing the complement — "from ancient memory" — before the subject, and that small deviation completely changed the effect of the text.

"Hyperbaton isn't a gratuitous ornament," Aurelio finally wrote in his review, "but a decision that forces the reader to experience first the sensation of antiquity, of distance, before even knowing what is emerging from it. The reader is submerged in the memory before knowing its content." Pleased with the sentence, he poured himself another coffee and kept picking apart the rest of the paragraph, finding in every syntactic inversion a rhetorical intention a distracted reader would never notice.

His editor, Carolina, stopped by his office around noon and found Aurelio surrounded by pages underlined in at least three different colors. "Still on the same sentence?" she asked, half amused, half worried. "It's not the same sentence," Aurelio replied, without looking up, "it's the sentence that explains the whole novel. If you understand why the author put 'from ancient memory' at the beginning and not at the end, you understand why the entire book moves backward in time instead of forward." Carolina, used to Aurelio's obsession with details, smiled and let him work.

When he finally turned in the review, two days later, it included a four-page analysis devoted entirely to the hyperbaton in the first three chapters. Some readers of the newspaper would complain, as always, that Aurelio overcomplicated things. But for those who truly loved literature, that review would be, for months, the best guide available for understanding why a seemingly simple novel hid, in the very order of its words, the entire architecture of its meaning.

## English Translation

At seventy-four, doña Clemencia decided she would leave her story in no one else's hands: she would write it herself, and she would write it in verse, the way the poets she had admired her whole life used to. "Anyone can write prose," she told her son Rodrigo when he asked her, somewhat worried, why she didn't simply tell her memories the way everyone else does. "I want my grandchildren, once I'm gone, to feel life, not just read about it."

She sat down every morning, for months, with a notebook and a pen that had belonged to her late husband, and began turning the loose memories of her childhood into carefully crafted verses. Instead of simply writing "my father worked the land from dawn on," Clemencia crossed out the line three times until she arrived at: "From the dawn, amid furrows and silence, my father consecrated the hours to the land." She smiled rereading it: the inverted order, with "from the dawn" up front, made the reader feel the weight of time first, before even knowing who was being spoken of.

Rodrigo, who had doubted the project at first, started visiting her every Sunday to hear the new verses. "Mom, nobody writes like that anymore," he remarked one afternoon, half joking, half worried the style would push away her future readers. Clemencia looked at him over her glasses. "That's precisely why I write it this way," she replied. "Anyone can tell what happened. Few dare to make the reader feel the order in which life was actually lived: the feeling first, the fact after."

When she finally finished the manuscript, almost two years after starting, she gathered the whole family to read them the last chapter, the one about her husband's death. "From the silence of the house, an enormous absence grew," she read, her voice steady despite the tears already welling up. "And in that emptiness his footsteps left behind, I learned, at last, to walk on my own." No one in the room said a word for a long moment; only the muffled sobs of her grandchildren could be heard, who for the first time understood, not with their heads but with their hearts, what their grandmother had lived through.

That night, while packing the manuscript into a wooden box to take to the printer, Clemencia thought that Rodrigo had been wrong: it didn't matter that no one else wrote this way anymore. What mattered was that, for the first time in her life, she had managed to say exactly what she felt, in the one order of words that did justice to her memories.

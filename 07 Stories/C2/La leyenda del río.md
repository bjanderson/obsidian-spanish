## English Translation

When the sun had set behind the hills, Grandmother Herminia brought two chairs out to the patio and sat down next to her granddaughter Camila, facing the river that ran gently past the foot of the house. "Grandma, why do those lights shine on the water?" asked the girl, pointing at some silvery glimmers that seemed to float on the current. The old woman smiled, like someone who had been waiting all afternoon for that question, and began to speak in the slow voice she saved for old stories.

"My grandparents' grandparents used to say that, long ago, this valley suffered a drought so cruel that the river shrank to a thread of mud. The fields cracked, the cattle died, and the whole village prayed to the sky for rain. A young woman named Inés, the humblest in the village, decided to climb alone to the river's source to beg for mercy. She walked for three days and three nights, and no sooner had she reached the spring than she came upon an old woman with silver hair who claimed to be the very spirit of the water."

"The old woman told her: 'I will return the flow to your village, but you must promise me that, every full moon, they will leave an offering of flowers on the bank, and that they will never defile these waters with words of hatred.' No sooner had she heard the condition than Inés accepted the deal without hesitation. As soon as she had spoken her promise, thunder rumbled through the hills and the river began to flow again, full and clear as never before."

"For generations, the village kept the promise. But a time came when the great-grandchildren of Inés's grandchildren forgot the story, and a proud young man, fed up with the old customs, mocked the ritual and threw stones into the water instead of flowers. No sooner had he finished laughing than the sky darkened and a flood swept away his lands. Since then, they say the river's spirit watches over it with those lights you see, and that it is only calmed when someone, with a sincere heart, offers it something beautiful."

Camila stayed there looking at the water, thoughtful. "So, are those lights the old woman of the river?" "No one knows for certain," her grandmother answered, "but just in case, tomorrow we'll cut the prettiest flowers from the garden to leave on the bank." The girl nodded, and from that night on she never looked at the river again without a certain reverent respect for whatever, they said, slept beneath its current.

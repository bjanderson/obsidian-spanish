## English Translation

Every two years, the Reyes family gathered at Grandmother Herminia's house, and every time it happened, the same living room filled with accents that seemed to belong to different languages, even though everyone was, in theory, speaking the same one. That year, relatives had arrived from Mexico, Argentina, Chile, and Puerto Rico, and no sooner had they sat down at the table than the misunderstandings began.

"Can someone pass me the popote?" asked Fernanda, the Mexican cousin, pointing at her glass of lemonade. Everyone stared at her, not understanding. "The what?" asked her Argentine uncle, Ignacio. "The popote, for drinking the juice," Fernanda clarified, a little confused by everyone else's confusion. Her Chilean cousin, Josefina, burst out laughing. "We call it a bombilla," she said, "although bombilla is also what we use to drink mate, so in Chile you have to clarify which bombilla you mean." Her Puerto Rican cousin, Daniel, shook his head. "On my island, that's called a sorbeto," he added, "and I'm sure in Argentina they call it something else entirely."

The confusion didn't end there. When Aunt Rosario, who had come from Ecuador, announced she had brought her guagua for everyone to meet, the Chilean cousin got excited thinking of a newborn baby, while the Puerto Rican uncle understood that Rosario had parked a bus outside the house. Only when Rosario, puzzled by everyone's confused faces, took out her phone and showed a photo of a smiling baby did the misunderstanding get resolved: in her country, guagua really did mean baby, but on the Caribbean coast, the same word meant a bus.

Grandmother Herminia, who had spent her whole life listening to her family scatter across different countries and return every two years with a renewed vocabulary, decided to step in with the wisdom of someone who had mediated hundreds of these conversations. "In this house," she announced, raising her glass, "when someone doesn't understand a word, instead of laughing, let them ask what it means in the speaker's country. That way, in a single afternoon, we learn vocabulary from half a continent." Everyone laughed, nodding, and for the rest of the meal, every new regional word that came up — chévere, pololo, guagua, popote, carro, auto — became a small shared lesson instead of a reason for teasing.

At the end of the night, as they said their goodbyes, Fernanda confessed to her grandmother that, even though she had been living abroad for several years, she had never felt as much pride in speaking Spanish as she did that night, surrounded by cousins who said the same things with completely different words. "That's the beautiful thing about Spanish," her grandmother replied, smiling. "It's not one language, it's hundreds of languages that understand each other."

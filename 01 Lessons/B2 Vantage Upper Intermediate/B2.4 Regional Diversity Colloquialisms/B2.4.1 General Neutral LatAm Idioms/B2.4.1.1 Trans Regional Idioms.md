## Explanation

Every language has expressions that don't translate word for word — you
have to know what they *mean* as a whole. Spanish has plenty of these, and
the good news is that a handful of them show up everywhere: from Mexico to
Argentina to Spain, the same idiom pops up with barely any variation.
These are your highest-value idioms to learn first, since they'll be
understood by almost any Spanish speaker you talk to, not just people from
one country.

An idiom (*modismo* or *expresión idiomática*) is a fixed phrase whose
meaning isn't obvious from its individual words. *Dar en el clavo*
literally means "to hit the nail" — but nobody's talking about carpentry;
it means to get something exactly right, like hitting the nail on the
head in English. Learning idioms like this is what makes your Spanish
sound natural instead of just technically correct.

## Key Points / Conjugation / Rules

- **Idioms are fixed** — you generally can't swap out words inside them.
  *Dar en el clavo* works; *dar en el tornillo* (screw) does not, even
  though a screw is a similar object.
- **Meaning comes from the whole phrase, not the individual words** —
  translating literally will usually confuse a native speaker or just
  sound strange.
- **Many of the most common idioms cross borders freely.** Unlike slang
  (which is often country-specific — see [[B2.4.1.2 Universal Colloquial Vocabulary]]),
  idioms like *costar un ojo de la cara* (to cost an arm and a leg) or *ser
  pan comido* (to be a piece of cake) are recognized across nearly every
  Spanish-speaking country.
- **Some idioms have direct English equivalents**, which makes them easy
  to remember — *la gota que colmó el vaso* is exactly "the straw that
  broke the camel's back," just with a glass of water instead of a camel.
- **Others don't map onto English at all**, so you just have to memorize
  the whole phrase as a unit — *no tener vela en este entierro* ("to have
  no candle in this burial") means it's none of your business or you have
  no say in the matter.
- **Not every idiom here is equally neutral** — most cross borders freely,
  but *tener mala leche* leans more toward Spain than Latin America;
  you'll still be understood, but *estar de malas* is the safer everyday
  choice in most Latin American countries.

## Examples

| Spanish | English | Notes |
| ------- | ------- | ----- |
| Dar en el clavo | To hit the nail on the head | Getting something exactly right |
| Tener mala leche | To have bad luck / to have a bad attitude | More common in Spain; in most of Latin America, *estar de malas* or *tener mal genio* is the more typical way to say it |
| El mundo es un pañuelo | It's a small world | Said when you unexpectedly run into someone you know |
| Costar un ojo de la cara | To cost an arm and a leg | Something very expensive |
| Ser pan comido | To be a piece of cake | Something very easy |
| Hablando del rey de Roma (, por la puerta se asoma) | Speak of the devil | Said when someone you were just talking about shows up |
| No tener vela en este entierro | To have no say in the matter | Literally "to have no candle at this burial" |
| La gota que colmó el vaso | The straw that broke the camel's back | The final small thing that makes a bad situation unbearable |
| Hacer una montaña de un grano de arena | To make a mountain out of a molehill | Overreacting to something small |

## Common Mistakes

- Translating idioms word for word: *cost an eye of the face* means
  nothing in English, just like *dar en el tornillo* means nothing in
  Spanish. Learn the whole phrase, not the individual words.
- Changing a word inside a fixed idiom: *tener mala leche* can't become
  *tener mala bebida* — idioms don't tolerate substitution the way regular
  sentences do.
- Assuming every idiom is safe in any context. *Tener mala leche* to
  describe a person's personality is fairly informal — it works fine with
  friends, but soften it in a professional setting.
- Confusing a trans-regional idiom with local slang. *Dar en el clavo* is
  understood everywhere; a slang word like a country-specific term for
  "cool" (see [[B2.4.1.2 Universal Colloquial Vocabulary]]) may only be
  understood in one country.

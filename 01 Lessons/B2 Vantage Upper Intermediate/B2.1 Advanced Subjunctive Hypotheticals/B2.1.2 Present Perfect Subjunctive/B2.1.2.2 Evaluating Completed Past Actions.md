## Explanation

Now that you can build the present perfect subjunctive, the real question
is *when* you'd actually reach for it. The short answer: any time you'd
use a present-tense subjunctive trigger — hoping, doubting, feeling glad or
sorry, judging something as good or bad — but the action you're reacting to
has **already happened**. You're not commenting on an action in progress;
you're evaluating something that's already done, from right now: *Espero
que hayas llegado bien* (I hope you arrived okay).

## Key Points / Conjugation / Rules

- **Hope about a finished action**: *esperar que* + present perfect
  subjunctive checks in on something that should already be complete:
  *Espero que hayas dormido bien* (I hope you slept well).
- **Doubt about a finished action**: *dudar que*, *no creer que*, and
  similar doubt triggers work the same way when questioning whether
  something already happened: *Dudo que haya terminado tan rápido* (I
  doubt he finished that quickly).
- **Emotional reactions** — glad, sorry, surprised, relieved — commonly
  pair with the present perfect subjunctive to react to news you just
  learned: *Me alegra que hayas conseguido el puesto* (I'm glad you got the
  position); *Siento que hayan perdido el vuelo* (I'm sorry you missed the
  flight).
- **Value judgments** with impersonal expressions — *es bueno que*, *es
  una lástima que*, *qué extraño que* — evaluate a completed action as
  good, unfortunate, or strange: *Es una lástima que no hayan podido
  venir* (It's a shame they weren't able to come).
- The key test: if you can imagine yourself saying it with a nod to
  something that just wrapped up — not something still happening, and not
  a distant memory — the present perfect subjunctive almost always fits.

## Examples

| Spanish                                            | English                                              | Notes                       |
| ---------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------- |
| Espero que hayas llegado bien.                       | I hope you arrived okay.                                  | hope about finished action              |
| Dudo que haya leído todo el libro en un día.         | I doubt he read the whole book in a day.                    | doubt about finished action               |
| Me alegra que hayan encontrado las llaves.           | I'm glad they found the keys.                                 | emotional reaction                          |
| Es una lástima que no hayas podido venir a la boda.  | It's a shame you weren't able to come to the wedding.          | value judgment                                |
| Qué bueno que hayamos terminado antes de la lluvia.  | It's great that we finished before the rain.                     | value judgment                                  |
| Siento que hayas tenido un día tan difícil.          | I'm sorry you've had such a hard day.                              | emotional reaction                                |

## Common Mistakes

- Using the present perfect **indicative** after a trigger that calls for
  the subjunctive: *Espero que has llegado bien* is wrong — the trigger
  *esperar que* needs the subjunctive: *Espero que hayas llegado bien.*
- Reaching for the imperfect subjunctive when the action is recent and
  still relevant: *Me alegra que consiguieras el puesto* sounds like a
  distant memory; for news you just heard, the present perfect subjunctive
  fits better: *Me alegra que hayas conseguido el puesto.*
- Skipping the subjunctive entirely with a value judgment: *Es una lástima
  que no has podido venir* is wrong — impersonal expressions of judgment
  are subjunctive triggers: *Es una lástima que no hayas podido venir.*
- Overusing the imperfect subjunctive for anything in the past: not every
  past action calls for the imperfect subjunctive — if it's a recently
  completed action you're reacting to right now, the present perfect
  subjunctive is usually the better fit.

## Explanation

You've built unreal conditions about right now — "if I had more money, I
would travel." This final pattern pushes that same idea into the past:
"if I had known, I would have told you." You're not imagining an
alternate present anymore; you're imagining an alternate **past** — a
different outcome for something that's already over and can't be changed.
Every piece of this pattern is one you already know: the pluperfect
subjunctive and the conditional perfect, just combined.

## Key Points / Conjugation / Rules

- **The pattern**: *si* + **pluperfect subjunctive** → **conditional
  perfect**. The *si* clause imagines a different past; the result clause
  says what would have happened: *Si hubiera sabido la verdad, te lo habría
  dicho* (If I had known the truth, I would have told you).
- The **conditional perfect** is built from [conditional of *haber*] +
  [past participle]: *habría, habrías, habría, habríamos, habrían* +
  participle — the mirror image of the pluperfect subjunctive's *hubiera*
  + participle, just with a different form of *haber*.
- This pattern describes something **already finished and unchangeable** —
  the classic structure behind "would have," "could have," "should have"
  regrets: *Si hubiéramos salido antes, no habríamos perdido el vuelo* (If
  we had left earlier, we wouldn't have missed the flight).
- In relaxed, everyday speech, many speakers use the **pluperfect
  subjunctive in both halves** instead of switching to the conditional
  perfect: *Si hubiéramos salido antes, no hubiéramos perdido el vuelo.*
  Both versions are heard constantly; the *habría* form is considered the
  more standard, textbook version.
- Don't confuse this with the **present** unreal conditional: if the
  situation is still ongoing or general, use *si* + imperfect subjunctive →
  conditional instead (previous lesson). This pattern is specifically for
  something in the finished past.

## Common Mistakes

- Using the simple conditional instead of the conditional perfect in the
  result clause: *Si hubiera sabido, te lo diría* mixes a past premise
  with a present-tense result — for a finished past, both halves need the
  perfect forms: *Si hubiera sabido, te lo habría dicho.*
- Using the imperfect subjunctive instead of the pluperfect in the *si*
  clause: *Si supiera la verdad, te lo habría dicho* mismatches present
  and past — for an unreal past premise, the *si* clause needs *hubiera*:
  *Si hubiera sabido la verdad, te lo habría dicho.*
- Forgetting that both halves point to something already over: if the
  situation could still play out, this isn't the right pattern — reach for
  the present unreal conditional (*si* + imperfect subjunctive →
  conditional) instead.
- Assuming *habría* is the only correct option: swapping in *hubiera* for
  the result clause too (*Si hubiera sabido, se lo hubiera dicho*) is
  extremely common in everyday speech and not an error — just less
  formal than the *habría* version.

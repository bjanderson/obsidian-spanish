## Explanation

In an active sentence, the subject does the action: *El chef preparó la
cena* (The chef prepared dinner). Sometimes you want to flip the focus onto
what happened rather than who did it — that's the **passive voice**: *La
cena fue preparada por el chef* (Dinner was prepared by the chef). Spanish
builds this the same basic way English does: a form of *ser*, a past
participle describing the action, and an optional *por* phrase naming who
did it.

## Key Points / Conjugation / Rules

- **The formula**: [subject] + [*ser*, conjugated in the tense of the
  original action] + [past participle] + [*por* + agent, optional]. The
  subject of the passive sentence is the thing or person the action
  happened **to**, not the one who did it.
- **The past participle agrees** in gender and number with the subject —
  unlike the perfect tenses you've learned, where the participle never
  changes: *El libro fue escrito por ella* (masculine singular) vs. *Las
  cartas fueron escritas por ella* (feminine plural) — *escrito* becomes
  *escritas* to match *las cartas*.
- **Ser matches the tense of the original active sentence**: if the active
  version is in the preterite, so is *ser* in the passive: *El premio fue
  otorgado ayer* (The prize was awarded yesterday). If it's in the present,
  *ser* stays present: *La casa es construida con materiales locales*
  (The house is built with local materials).
- **The *por* phrase is optional** — include it only when who did the
  action matters or is worth naming: *El cuadro fue pintado por Picasso*
  names the painter; *El cuadro fue pintado en 1937* leaves the painter out
  entirely and just gives a date instead.
- This is the passive voice you'd find in **news writing, history,
  academic text** — formal, written registers. The next lesson covers how
  Spanish speakers usually express this same idea out loud instead.

## Examples

| Spanish                                                    | English                                                | Notes                            |
| ------------------------------------------------------------ | ----------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------- |
| La cena fue preparada por el chef.                             | Dinner was prepared by the chef.                                | *fue* + *preparada* (fem. sg.)             |
| Los libros fueron escritos por varios autores.                 | The books were written by several authors.                        | *fueron* + *escritos* (masc. pl.)            |
| El premio es otorgado cada año por el comité.                  | The prize is awarded every year by the committee.                    | present tense passive                          |
| Las casas serán construidas con materiales locales.            | The houses will be built with local materials.                        | future tense passive                             |
| El puente fue diseñado por un ingeniero famoso.                | The bridge was designed by a famous engineer.                           | *fue* + *diseñado* (masc. sg.)                     |
| La ley fue aprobada por el congreso el mes pasado.             | The law was approved by congress last month.                             | *fue* + *aprobada* (fem. sg.)                        |

## Common Mistakes

- Forgetting participle agreement: *La carta fue escrito* is wrong — the
  participle must match the feminine singular subject: *La carta fue
  escrita.*
- Using *estar* instead of *ser* for this construction: *La cena estuvo
  preparada por el chef* describes a resulting state, not the passive
  action itself — for the passive voice, *ser* is what's needed: *La cena
  fue preparada por el chef.*
- Mismatching the tense of *ser* with the original action: if the event
  happened in the past, *ser* should too — *El premio es otorgado ayer* is
  wrong; it should be *El premio fue otorgado ayer.*
- Overusing this construction in everyday speech: this *ser* passive is
  correct but sounds formal and written — native speakers reach for other
  structures (see the next lesson) far more often in conversation.

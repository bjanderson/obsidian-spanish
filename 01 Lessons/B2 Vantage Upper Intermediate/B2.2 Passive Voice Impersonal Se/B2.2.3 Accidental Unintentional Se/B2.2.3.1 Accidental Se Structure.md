## Explanation

In English, "I forgot my phone" puts the blame squarely on you — you're the
one who forgot. Spanish has a clever way to describe the exact same event
while making it sound like it just *happened* to you, almost by accident:
*Se me olvidó el celular* (literally "The phone forgot-itself to me").
This is the **accidental *se***, and it's the natural, everyday way
Spanish speakers talk about things they dropped, broke, lost, or forgot.

## Key Points / Conjugation / Rules

- **The formula** always has four parts, in order: *se*, then a pronoun
  for who it happened to (*me, te, le, nos, les*), then the verb, then the
  thing that got dropped, lost, or broken, acting as the grammatical
  subject. The verb agrees with **the thing**, not with the person: *Se
  me cayó el vaso* (I dropped the glass — singular *vaso*, singular
  *cayó*); *Se me cayeron los platos* (I dropped the plates — plural
  *platos*, plural *cayeron*).
- **The indirect object pronoun identifies who it happened to**: *me* (to
  me), *te* (to you), *le* (to him/her/you formal), *nos* (to us), *les*
  (to them/you all). Since *le* and *les* are ambiguous on their own, you
  can clarify with *a + person*: *A Javier se le rompieron los
  audífonos* (Javier's headphones broke).
- **Common verbs** in this pattern: *olvidarse* (to forget), *caerse* (to
  fall/drop), *romperse* (to break), *perderse* (to lose), *acabarse* (to
  run out of). Each pairs naturally with this structure to shift the
  blame away from the person: *se me olvidó, se me cayó, se me rompió, se
  me perdió, se me acabó.*
- **Word order matters**: *se* + pronoun + verb always comes first, and
  the thing involved (the grammatical subject) comes after: *Se le
  perdió el pasaporte a María* (María lost her passport), not *El
  pasaporte se le perdió a María* — both are technically possible, but
  the first order is far more natural.
- This construction softens responsibility — it's not that you're lying
  about what happened, it's that Spanish has a built-in way to frame an
  accident as something that happened *to* you rather than something you
  *did*.

## Examples

| Spanish                                          | English                                        | Notes                       |
| ---------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------- |
| Se me olvidó el celular en casa.                     | I forgot my cellphone at home.                            | *se me* + *olvidó*, singular subject    |
| A ella se le olvidaron las llaves en el trabajo.     | She forgot her keys at work.                                | *se le* + *olvidaron*, plural subject     |
| Se te cayó la tarjeta de crédito.                    | You dropped your credit card.                                | *se te* + *cayó*, singular subject          |
| A ellos se les cayeron las cámaras.                  | They dropped their cameras.                                   | *se les* + *cayeron*, plural subject          |
| Se nos rompió la ventana.                            | Our window broke.                                                | *se nos* + *rompió*, singular subject           |
| A María se le perdió el pasaporte en el aeropuerto.  | María lost her passport at the airport.                            | *se le* + *perdió*, singular subject              |

## Common Mistakes

- Matching the verb to the person instead of the thing: *Se me cayeron el
  vaso* is wrong — the verb agrees with *el vaso* (singular), not with
  *me*: *Se me cayó el vaso.*
- Forgetting the indirect object pronoun entirely: *Se olvidó el celular*
  drops who it happened to — for the accidental construction, the pronoun
  is required: *Se me olvidó el celular.*
- Leaving *le*/*les* ambiguous without context: *Se le rompió el vaso*
  doesn't say whose glass broke — add *a + name* when it's not already
  clear: *A Javier se le rompió el vaso.*
- Treating this as optional flavor rather than the natural default: this
  is genuinely how native speakers talk about accidents — reaching for
  *Yo perdí mi pasaporte* instead isn't wrong, but *Se me perdió el
  pasaporte* is what you'll hear far more often.

## Explanation

Imagine someone asks you, "Is María smart?" In English, you'd say "Yes,
she is" — and that "is" is doing double duty, standing in for "smart"
without repeating the word. Spanish does something very similar with
*lo*: *¿María es inteligente? Sí, lo es.* Here, *lo* is picking up the
adjective *inteligente* from the question so you don't have to say it
again.

This use of *lo* — sometimes called "resumptive lo" — is one of the most
common ways Spanish avoids repetition, and it sounds much more natural
than repeating the adjective every time.

## Key Points / Conjugation / Rules

- **Lo picks up an adjective already mentioned**, standing in for the
  quality or characteristic itself: *María es muy inteligente. Sí, lo
  es* (María is very intelligent. Yes, she is).
- **Lo never changes for gender or number**, even though the adjective
  it's replacing might have been feminine or plural: *Juan y Carlos son
  muy guapos. Sí, lo son* (Juan and Carlos are very handsome. Yes, they
  are) — *lo* stays *lo* even though *guapos* is masculine plural.
- **Lo can also pick up an adverb of manner**, standing in for the way
  something is done — though this is less common than with adjectives:
  *¿Explicas bien los conceptos? Sí, lo hago* (Do you explain the
  concepts well? Yes, I do).
- **This doesn't work naturally with every adverb** — when the focus is
  on something like speed, native speakers usually just repeat the
  adverb instead: *¿Corres muy rápido? Sí, corro muy rápido* sounds more
  natural than forcing *lo* into that answer.
- **Lo is often skipped in short, factual statements** about
  professions, nationalities, or religion: *¿Juan es católico? Sí, es*
  (not *Sí, lo es*). But once you add a quality to that role, *lo*
  becomes natural again: *¿Es un buen católico? Sí, lo es.*

## Examples

| Spanish | English | Notes |
| ------- | ------- | ----- |
| María es muy inteligente. Sí, lo es. | María is very intelligent. Yes, she is. | Lo picks up inteligente |
| Juan y Carlos son muy guapos. Sí, lo son. | Juan and Carlos are very handsome. Yes, they are. | Lo stays the same despite masculine plural adjective |
| ¿Es aburrida la película? No, no lo es. | Is the movie boring? No, it isn't. | Lo picks up aburrida |
| ¿Son cómodos los zapatos? No, no lo son. | Are the shoes comfortable? No, they aren't. | Lo stays the same despite plural adjective |
| ¿Explicas bien los conceptos? Sí, lo hago. | Do you explain the concepts well? Yes, I do. | Lo picks up the adverb bien |
| ¿Juan es profesor? No, no es. | Is Juan a teacher? No, he isn't. | Lo often dropped for short factual statements |
| ¿Es un buen profesor? Sí, lo es. | Is he a good teacher? Yes, he is. | Lo returns once a quality is added |

## Common Mistakes

- Making *lo* agree with the gender or number of the adjective it
  replaces — it always stays *lo*, even for feminine or plural subjects.
- Forcing *lo* onto every adverb answer. With adverbs about speed or
  similar qualities, repeating the adverb usually sounds more natural
  than using *lo hago*.
- Adding *lo* to a short factual statement about profession, nationality,
  or religion when nothing else is said: *Sí, lo es* sounds odd as a bare
  answer to "¿Es mexicana?" — plain *Sí, es* is more natural there.
- Confusing this resumptive *lo* with the direct-object *lo* that
  replaces a specific masculine noun — see
  [[B2.5.1.1 Lo Replacing Masculine Nouns and Ideas]] for that related
  but distinct use.

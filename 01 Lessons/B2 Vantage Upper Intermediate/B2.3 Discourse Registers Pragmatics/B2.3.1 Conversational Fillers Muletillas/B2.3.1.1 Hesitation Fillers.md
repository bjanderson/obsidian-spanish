## Explanation

Think about how much English relies on "um," "like," "you know," and "I
mean" to keep a sentence flowing while your brain catches up. Spanish has
its own toolkit of these little words — called **muletillas** — and
knowing them does two things at once: it buys you thinking time, and it
makes you sound noticeably more like a native speaker instead of someone
reciting textbook sentences.

## Key Points / Conjugation / Rules

- **Este...** works like English "um" — a pure hesitation sound while you
  think of the next word: *Quiero... este... pedir un café* (I want... um...
  to order a coffee).
- **Bueno** softens a transition or introduces a response, similar to
  "well" in English: *Bueno, no estoy seguro* (Well, I'm not sure);
  *Bueno, empecemos* (Well, let's begin).
- **O sea** rephrases or clarifies what you just said, like "I mean" or
  "that is to say": *Llego tarde, o sea, como siempre* (I'm running late,
  I mean, like always).
- **Es decir** is a slightly more formal cousin of *o sea*, also meaning
  "that is to say" or "in other words," often used to restate something
  more precisely: *Trabajo desde casa, es decir, soy independiente* (I work
  from home, that is, I'm self-employed).
- Two set phrases worth memorizing exactly as-is: ***que yo sepa*** (as
  far as I know) and ***que yo recuerde*** (as far as I remember) — both
  use the subjunctive as a fixed expression, not because of any trigger you
  need to reason through: *¿Dónde vive Juan? Que yo sepa, vive en
  Guadalajara* (Where does Juan live? As far as I know, he lives in
  Guadalajara).

## Examples

| Spanish                                                    | English                                            | Notes                          |
| ------------------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------ |
| Quiero... este... pedir la cuenta.                              | I want... um... to ask for the check.                       | *este*, hesitation filler                |
| Bueno, no sé qué decirte.                                       | Well, I don't know what to tell you.                          | *bueno*, softening transition              |
| Llegamos tarde, o sea, como siempre.                            | We arrived late, I mean, like always.                           | *o sea*, rephrasing                          |
| Estudio por las noches, es decir, después del trabajo.          | I study at night, that is, after work.                            | *es decir*, precise restatement                |
| Que yo sepa, la reunión es a las tres.                          | As far as I know, the meeting is at three.                          | *que yo sepa*, fixed expression                  |
| Que yo recuerde, nunca hablamos de eso.                         | As far as I remember, we never talked about that.                     | *que yo recuerde*, fixed expression                |

## Common Mistakes

- Avoiding fillers entirely to sound "correct": speech with zero
  hesitation markers can sound stiff and over-rehearsed — natural Spanish
  uses these constantly, just like natural English does.
- Overusing a single filler until it becomes a verbal tic: mixing *este*,
  *bueno*, and *o sea* sounds more natural than repeating just one of them
  in every sentence.
- Treating *o sea* and *es decir* as interchangeable in every context: *o
  sea* is more casual and conversational; *es decir* leans slightly more
  formal — both work, but the register shifts a little.
- Trying to reason out why *que yo sepa* uses the subjunctive: it's a fixed
  expression, not a trigger-based construction — memorize the whole chunk
  rather than analyzing it grammatically each time.

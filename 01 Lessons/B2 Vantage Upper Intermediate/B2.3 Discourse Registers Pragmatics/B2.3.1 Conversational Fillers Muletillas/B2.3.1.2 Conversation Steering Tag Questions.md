## Explanation

English tacks little check-ins onto the end of statements — "isn't it?",
"right?", "don't you think?" — that invite the listener to agree or
respond. Spanish does something similar but far simpler: pick a short tag,
stick it at the end of almost any statement, and you've turned it into an
invitation to engage. Unlike English, you don't need to match the verb or
tense at all — the tag stays exactly the same no matter what came before
it.

## Key Points / Conjugation / Rules

- **¿Verdad?** ("true?") is the most universal, all-purpose tag — it works
  after any statement, in any register, and is understood everywhere:
  *Vives cerca de aquí, ¿verdad?* (You live nearby, right?).
- **¿No?** is the most casual, everyday version — functionally identical
  to *¿verdad?* but a bit more informal: *Hace calor hoy, ¿no?* (It's hot
  today, isn't it?).
- **¿Cierto?** ("certain?") works the same way and is especially common in
  parts of South America — a solid alternative to *¿verdad?* with the
  same meaning: *Ya terminaste, ¿cierto?* (You already finished, right?).
- **¿Ya?** functions a little differently — it checks in on whether
  something has happened yet, or confirms understanding/readiness in
  context: *Te espero afuera, ¿ya?* (I'll wait for you outside, okay?);
  *¿Ya entendiste?* (Do you get it now?).
- **No polarity-flipping needed**: English switches "isn't it" after a
  positive statement to "is it" after a negative one — Spanish doesn't do
  this. *¿Verdad?* and *¿no?* work identically after both positive and
  negative statements: *No vienes hoy, ¿verdad?* (You're not coming today,
  right?).

## Examples

| Spanish                                       | English                                          | Notes                       |
| -------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------- |
| Vives cerca de aquí, ¿verdad?                       | You live nearby, right?                              | *¿verdad?*, universal tag              |
| Hace calor hoy, ¿no?                                | It's hot today, isn't it?                              | *¿no?*, casual tag                       |
| Ya terminaste el proyecto, ¿cierto?                 | You already finished the project, right?               | *¿cierto?*, regional alternative           |
| Te espero afuera, ¿ya?                              | I'll wait for you outside, okay?                          | *¿ya?*, checking in                          |
| No vienes hoy, ¿verdad?                             | You're not coming today, right?                            | *¿verdad?* after a negative statement          |
| Esto no está bien, ¿no?                             | This isn't right, is it?                                     | *¿no?* after a negative statement                |

## Common Mistakes

- Trying to flip polarity like English does: adding a positive tag after a
  negative statement ("isn't it" logic) isn't how Spanish works — *¿verdad?*
  and *¿no?* stay the same regardless of the statement's polarity.
- Matching the tag to the verb tense: unlike a full question, these tags
  never conjugate or change form to match what came before — *¿verdad?*
  works after present, past, future, any tense at all.
- Using *¿ya?* interchangeably with *¿verdad?*: *¿ya?* leans toward
  checking completion or readiness, not general agreement-seeking — mixing
  them up can sound slightly off.
- Overusing a single tag: like fillers, leaning on just one tag
  constantly can sound repetitive — native speakers mix *¿verdad?*, *¿no?*,
  and *¿cierto?* depending on the moment and region.

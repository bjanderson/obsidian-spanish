## Explanation

Flat-out disagreeing — "No, you're wrong" — can shut a conversation down
fast, even when you have a fair point to make. Spanish has a set of go-to
phrases for pushing back gently: acknowledging the other person's point
first, then offering your own take without dismissing theirs. It's the
same diplomatic move English makes with "I see what you mean, but..." —
Spanish just has its own well-worn versions of it.

## Key Points / Conjugation / Rules

- **Entiendo tu punto, pero...** ("I understand your point, but...")
  validates the other person before you disagree: *Entiendo tu punto, pero
  no estoy totalmente de acuerdo* (I understand your point, but I don't
  fully agree).
- **Tienes razón en parte, pero...** ("You're partly right, but...")
  concedes something real before adding a counterpoint: *Tienes razón en
  parte, pero creo que también hay otros factores* (You're partly right,
  but I think there are other factors too).
- **Desde mi perspectiva / Desde mi punto de vista** ("From my
  perspective / From my point of view") frames what follows as your
  personal take, not a claim about absolute truth — a softer way to
  disagree: *Desde mi perspectiva, esa decisión fue un error* (From my
  perspective, that decision was a mistake).
- **Es posible, pero...** ("That's possible, but...") leaves room for the
  other person's idea while still pushing back: *Es posible, pero no
  creo que sea la mejor opción* (That's possible, but I don't think it's
  the best option).
- **Por un lado... por otro lado...** ("On one hand... on the other
  hand...") lets you present both sides of an issue, useful for staying
  neutral or building up to a nuanced opinion: *Por un lado, entiendo la
  regla; por otro lado, me parece injusta* (On one hand, I understand the
  rule; on the other hand, it seems unfair to me).

## Common Mistakes

- Jumping straight to "no" without acknowledging the other person's
  point: *No, estás equivocado* skips the diplomatic step — starting with
  *Entiendo tu punto, pero...* keeps the conversation open instead of
  shutting it down.
- Treating *desde mi perspectiva* as a hedge that weakens your opinion: it
  actually does the opposite — it frames your view as a legitimate,
  personal stance, not a wishy-washy guess.
- Overusing *pero* without the acknowledgment that should come before it:
  the softening effect comes from validating first — *pero* alone,
  without *entiendo tu punto* or *tienes razón en parte* leading into it,
  loses that effect.
- Forgetting these phrases work for genuine partial agreement, not just
  disagreement dressed up politely: *tienes razón en parte* should
  reflect something you actually agree with, not just a rhetorical move.

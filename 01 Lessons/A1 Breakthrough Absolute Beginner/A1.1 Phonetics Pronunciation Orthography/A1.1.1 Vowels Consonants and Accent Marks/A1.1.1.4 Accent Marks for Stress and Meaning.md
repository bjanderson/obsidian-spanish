## Explanation

Every Spanish word of more than one syllable has one syllable that gets a
little more punch than the rest — the stressed syllable. Spanish spelling
has two ways to show you which syllable that is: a set of default stress
rules, and a written accent mark (called a *tilde*) that overrides those
rules whenever a word doesn't follow them.

**The default rules, when there is no written accent:**

- If a word ends in a vowel, or in *n* or *s*, stress falls on the
  second-to-last syllable. *Ca-sa*, *co-men*, *li-bros* — all stressed on
  the next-to-last syllable, no accent mark needed.
- If a word ends in any other consonant, stress falls on the last syllable.
  *Ha-blar*, *re-loj*, *ciu-dad* — all stressed on the final syllable, no
  accent mark needed.

**When a word breaks one of these rules, it needs a written accent** on the
stressed vowel to show where the stress really falls. *Ha-bló* ends in a
vowel, so the default rule would put stress on *ha-*, but the word is
actually stressed on *-bló* — so it gets a written accent to say so.

Spanish also uses the accent mark to tell apart two words that are spelled
and pronounced almost the same but mean completely different things — most
famously *el* (the) versus *él* (he/him). Neither word breaks a stress rule
on its own; the accent here exists purely to separate meanings, not to mark
stress. A handful of common word pairs work this way, and it's worth
learning them as a set.

## Key Points / Conjugation / Rules

- Words ending in a vowel, *n*, or *s* stress the second-to-last syllable by
  default — no accent needed.
- Words ending in any other consonant stress the last syllable by
  default — no accent needed.
- A written accent overrides the default rule and marks exactly where the
  real stress falls.
- A written accent can also separate two otherwise-identical words by
  meaning, even when stress isn't the issue — *el* vs. *él*, *tu* vs. *tú*,
  *mi* vs. *mí*, *si* vs. *sí*, *te* vs. *té*, *mas* vs. *más*.
- Accent marks only ever sit on a vowel (*á, é, í, ó, ú*) — never on a
  consonant.

## Examples

| Spanish | English         | Notes                                                          |
| ------- | --------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------- |
| árbol   | tree             | Ends in a consonant other than *n/s*, but stress is on the first syllable — breaks the default rule, so it needs the accent. |
| rápido  | fast             | Ends in a vowel, so the default rule expects stress on the second-to-last syllable — but it's actually on the first, so it needs the accent. |
| hablo / habló | I speak / he spoke | Same letters, different stressed syllable — the accent is the only difference and it changes the meaning. |
| el gato | the cat          | *el* (no accent) is the article "the."                          |
| él tiene un gato | he has a cat | *él* (with accent) is the pronoun "he."                          |
| ¿Tú tienes tiempo? | Do you have time? | *tú* (with accent) is the pronoun "you"; *tu* (no accent) would mean "your." |

## Common Mistakes

- Dropping the accent on words like *rápido* or *árbol*, which changes where
  a reader expects the stress to fall.
- Writing *el* with an accent when it means "the" — the accent belongs only
  on *él* the pronoun.
- Assuming every stressed syllable needs a written accent — most Spanish
  words follow the default rules and need no accent at all.
- Placing an accent on a consonant, or on the wrong vowel in a diphthong —
  accents only ever go on one of the five vowels.

## Explanation

A tilde (written accent mark) appears over a vowel — *á, é, í, ó, ú* — to show
which syllable of a word carries the spoken stress. Later lessons (A1.1.2) cover
the exact rules for when a tilde is required. For now, the key idea is that a
tilde marks the stressed syllable, and adding or removing one can change a
word's meaning entirely, even though the spelling looks almost the same.

## Key Points / Conjugation / Rules

- A tilde over a vowel (*á, é, í, ó, ú*) marks the stressed syllable.
- Some short words use a tilde only to distinguish meaning from an otherwise
  identical word (*tú* "you" vs. *tu* "your"; *él* "he" vs. *el* "the"; *sí*
  "yes" vs. *si* "if").

## Examples

| Spanish   | English      | Notes                                    |
| --------- | ------------ | ------------------------------------------ |
| café      | coffee       | Tilde on *é* marks the stressed syllable. |
| teléfono  | telephone    | Stress falls on *lé*, not the last syllable. |
| país      | country      | Tilde breaks up what would sound like one syllable. |
| música    | music        | Stress falls three syllables from the end. |
| Ana lava el carro. | Ana washes the car. | No tildes needed — stress falls on the normal syllable. |
| ¿Tú vienes o vienes tú? | Are you coming? | *Tú* ("you") needs a tilde; *tu* ("your") does not. |

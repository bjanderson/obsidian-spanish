## Explanation

Most Spanish consonants sound close enough to their English counterparts
that you can guess them correctly. A handful do not, and those are the ones
that make a learner sound foreign — or change a word's meaning entirely —
when pronounced the English way. This lesson covers the consonants that
need their own rule: *c, z, g, j, h, r, rr,* and *ñ*.

- ***C*** changes sound based on the letter after it. Before *a, o, u*, it's a
  hard "k" sound, as in *casa*. Before *e* or *i*, most of Latin America
  pronounces it as a soft "s," as in *cielo* — so *cielo* sounds like "syelo,"
  not "kyelo."
- ***Z*** is pronounced like an English "s" throughout Latin America — *zapato*
  sounds like "sapato." (In Spain, *z* is pronounced like "th," but that form
  is not used in this course.)
- ***G*** works like *c*: hard (like the "g" in "go") before *a, o, u*, but a
  harsh, throaty sound before *e* or *i* — the same sound as *j* (see below).
  *Gato* keeps the hard "g," but *gente* does not.
- ***J*** is a harsh, throaty "h" sound, made further back in the throat than
  the English "h." Think of it like the "ch" in the Scottish word "loch," but
  shorter. *Jugo* sounds roughly like "HOO-goh," not "JOO-goh."
- ***H*** is always silent in Spanish. It is never pronounced, no matter what
  vowel follows it. *Hola* starts with the vowel sound, not an "h" sound.
- ***R*** in the middle of a word (not doubled) is a quick, single tap of the
  tongue against the ridge behind your top teeth — close to the "tt" sound
  in the American English word "butter." *Pero* uses this single tap.
- ***RR***, and a single *r* at the start of a word, is a trill: several rapid
  taps in a row, like a tiny motor. *Perro* uses this trill — and *pero* (but)
  versus *perro* (dog) is a real, meaning-changing contrast.
- ***Ñ*** is not an "n" with an accent — it's its own letter, pronounced like
  the "ny" in the English word "canyon." *Año* (year) sounds like "AH-nyoh,"
  completely different from *ano* (anus) without the tilde.

## Key Points / Conjugation / Rules

- *C* and *g* both split into a hard sound (before *a, o, u*) and a soft/throaty
  sound (before *e, i*) — the vowel that follows decides the sound.
- *Z* always sounds like "s" in this course's neutral Latin American Spanish.
- *J* is always the harsh, throaty sound — regardless of the vowel after it.
- *H* is always silent — never pronounced under any circumstance.
- A single *r* between vowels is a soft tap; *rr*, or *r* at the start of a
  word, is a full trill. The two are not interchangeable — they can change a
  word's meaning.
- *Ñ* is a distinct letter from *n*, not a decorated version of it.

## Examples

| Spanish | English   | Notes                                                         |
| ------- | --------- | -------------------------------------------------------------- |
| cielo   | sky       | *c* before *i* is a soft "s" sound, not "k."                    |
| gato    | cat       | *g* before *a* stays hard, like English "go."                   |
| gente   | people    | *g* before *e* becomes the throaty *j* sound, not hard "g."     |
| jugo    | juice     | *j* is always the harsh, throaty sound.                        |
| hora    | hour/time | *h* is silent — the word starts on the vowel sound.             |
| pero / perro | but / dog | Single tap *r* vs. trilled *rr* — the only difference between the two words. |
| año     | year      | *ñ* sounds like "ny," distinct from plain *n*.                  |

## Common Mistakes

- Pronouncing *z* like the "th" in "think" — that's the Spain pronunciation;
  this course uses the neutral Latin American "s" sound.
- Saying the *h* in words like *hola* or *hora* — it must stay silent.
- Using a hard "g" sound before *e* or *i* (as in "get"), instead of the
  throaty *j* sound Spanish uses there.
- Flattening *rr* into a single tap, so *perro* sounds like *pero* — the
  trill is not optional; it changes the word's meaning.
- Pronouncing *ñ* as a plain "n," which turns *año* (year) into a different,
  unrelated word.

## Explanation

A handful of Spanish consonants don't map cleanly onto English sounds, and a
couple of them even change their sound depending on which letter comes
right after them. Getting these right early makes a bigger difference to
how natural you sound than almost anything else at this stage.

**C and Z — both sound like "s" in Latin American Spanish.** Before *a, o,*
or *u*, *c* is a hard "k" sound (*casa, cosa*). But before *e* or *i*, *c*
sounds just like "s" (*cena, cielo*) — and *z* always sounds like "s," no
matter what follows (*zapato, taza*). This merger is called *seseo*, and
it's the standard, neutral pronunciation across Latin America. Spain uses a
"th" sound for these instead — that's a regional feature of European
Spanish, not something you need here.

**G has two sounds, and it depends on the vowel that follows.** Before *a,
o,* or *u*, *g* is a hard sound like the English "g" in "go" (*gato,
gusto*). Before *e* or *i*, it switches to the same harsh, breathy sound as
the letter *j* (*gente, gigante*) — this is why *g* can sound completely
different across two words that look similar.

**J is always that same harsh, breathy sound** — close to the English "h"
in "hot," but stronger — no matter which vowel follows (*jamón, jugo,
jefe*).

**H stays silent**, exactly as you saw in the alphabet lesson — it never
adds a sound of its own, even next to other tricky letters like *g* or *j*.

**R and RR are two different sounds, not one letter said louder.** A
single *r* between vowels is a quick, soft tap — closer to the "tt" sound
in the American pronunciation of "butter" than to an English "r" (*pero,
cara*). *RR*, and a single *r* at the start of a word, is a longer, rolled
trill (*perro, rosa*). Mixing these up can actually change a word's
meaning — *pero* (but) and *perro* (dog) are a classic example.

**Ñ is a distinct nasal sound**, close to the "ny" in "canyon" — not an
accented *n*, and not interchangeable with it (*año* vs. *ano*, as you saw
in the alphabet lesson).

## Key Points / Conjugation / Rules

- *C* before *e/i* and *z* in any position both sound like "s" — this is
  *seseo*, the neutral Latin American standard (not the Spain "th" sound).
- *G* before *a/o/u* is a hard "g"; before *e/i* it sounds like *j*.
- *J* always makes the harsh, breathy *jota* sound, regardless of the
  following vowel.
- *H* is always silent.
- A single *r* between vowels is a soft tap; *rr*, and *r* at the start of
  a word, is a rolled trill.
- *Ñ* is its own nasal sound, distinct from *n*.

## Examples

| Spanish | English | Notes |
| ------- | ------- | ----- |
| El cielo está despejado. | The sky is clear. | *Cielo* — *c* before *i* sounds like "s." |
| Necesito un vaso de agua. | I need a glass of water. | *Necesito* — the *c* before *i* sounds like "s," matching the *seseo* rule. |
| Mi hermano juega al fútbol. | My brother plays soccer. | *Juega* — *j* keeps its harsh sound before *u*. |
| La gente es muy amable aquí. | The people are very kind here. | *Gente* — *g* before *e* sounds like *j*, not like a hard "g." |
| Compré un carro nuevo. | I bought a new car. | *Carro* uses the rolled *rr* — different from *caro* (expensive). |
| Ella tiene un año más que yo. | She's one year older than me. | *Año* — the *ñ* is a distinct nasal sound, not an accented *n*. |

## Common Mistakes

- Pronouncing *c* and *z* with the Spain "th" sound instead of the neutral
  Latin American "s" sound — this vault teaches *seseo* as the standard.
- Using a hard "g" sound before *e* or *i* (as in English "get") instead
  of switching to the *jota* sound, e.g. saying *gente* like "GEN-teh"
  instead of "HEN-teh."
- Rolling every *r* like a double *rr*, or flattening every *rr* into a
  single soft tap — both change the word's meaning (*pero* vs. *perro*).
- Pronouncing the *h* in words where it sits next to another tricky letter,
  like *hijo* — the *h* is still silent even there.

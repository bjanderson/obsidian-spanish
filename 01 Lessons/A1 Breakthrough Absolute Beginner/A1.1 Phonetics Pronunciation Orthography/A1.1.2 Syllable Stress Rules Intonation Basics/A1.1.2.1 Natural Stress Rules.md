## Explanation

Every Spanish word longer than one syllable has one syllable that gets a
little more punch than the rest. Most of the time you don't need an accent
mark to know which one — Spanish spelling gives you a shortcut: look at the
last letter of the word.

**The rule has two branches:**

- If a word ends in a vowel, or in *n* or *s*, the stress falls on the
  second-to-last syllable. Think of *casa*, *comen*, *libros* — say them out
  loud and the punch naturally lands on the next-to-last beat.
- If a word ends in any other consonant (most commonly *l*, *r*, *d*, or
  *z*), the stress falls on the last syllable. *Hablar*, *reloj*, *ciudad* —
  the punch lands on the final beat.

This is the same rule you already met in [[A1.1.1.4 Accent Marks for Stress
and Meaning]] as the "default" a written accent overrides. Here the focus is
narrower: getting fast and automatic at applying it to brand-new words,
without needing to think about accent marks at all, because most Spanish
words never need one — they simply follow this pattern.

A practical trick: break the word into syllables first (count the vowel
sounds), then count backward from the end. Second-to-last syllable = vowel/
*n*/*s* ending. Last syllable = any other consonant ending. Practice this on
words you don't already know the pronunciation of — that's the real test of
whether the rule has become automatic.

## Key Points / Conjugation / Rules

- Word ends in a vowel, *n*, or *s* → stress the second-to-last syllable.
- Word ends in any other consonant → stress the last syllable.
- No written accent is needed when a word already follows its expected
  pattern — the accent in [[A1.1.1.4 Accent Marks for Stress and Meaning]]
  only shows up when a word breaks this rule.
- Count syllables by counting vowel sounds, not letters — *ciudad* has two
  syllables (*ciu-dad*), not four.
- This rule applies to the vast majority of Spanish words — learning it well
  lets you pronounce words you've never heard before with confidence.

## Examples

| Spanish  | English    | Notes                                                                          |
| -------- | ---------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| ca-sa    | house      | Ends in a vowel → stress on *ca-*, the second-to-last syllable.                |
| co-men   | they eat   | Ends in *n* → stress on *co-*, the second-to-last syllable.                    |
| li-bros  | books      | Ends in *s* → stress on *li-*, the second-to-last syllable.                    |
| ha-blar  | to speak   | Ends in *r* (not *n*/*s*) → stress on *-blar*, the last syllable.              |
| re-loj   | clock      | Ends in *j* → stress on *-loj*, the last syllable.                             |
| ciu-dad  | city       | Ends in *d* → stress on *-dad*, the last syllable.                             |
| pro-fe-sor | professor | Ends in *r* → stress on *-sor*, the last syllable, even though the word is longer. |

## Common Mistakes

- Stressing the last syllable of words ending in a vowel, *n*, or *s* out of
  habit from English, where stress placement is far less predictable.
- Miscounting syllables by counting letters instead of vowel sounds, which
  throws off where "second-to-last" actually lands.
- Forgetting that *y* at the end of a word (as in *hoy*, *muy*) counts as
  part of the final vowel sound, not a consonant, for stress purposes.
- Assuming every word needs a written accent — most don't, because they
  already follow this default pattern.

## Explanation

When two vowels sit next to each other in a Spanish word, they either blend
into one syllable or stay apart as two separate syllables — and which one
happens changes how you count syllables, and in turn where a word's natural
stress falls (see [[A1.1.2.1 Natural Stress Rules]]).

To tell them apart, Spanish vowels split into two groups:

- **Strong vowels**: *a*, *e*, *o*
- **Weak vowels**: *i*, *u*

**A diphthong (_diptongo_)** happens when a strong vowel pairs with a weak
vowel, or two weak vowels pair together, in either order. The two vowels
glide together into a single syllable. *Bien*, *cielo*, and *cuidado* each
have a diphthong — say them slowly and notice the two vowel sounds blend
into one beat.

**A hiatus (_hiato_)** happens when two strong vowels land next to each
other. Because both are strong, neither one gives way — they each keep
their own syllable. *Ca-os* (chaos) and *fe-o* (ugly) both split into two
separate syllables at the vowel boundary.

There's a third case worth knowing: when a weak vowel next to a strong
vowel carries a written accent, the accent breaks the glide on purpose and
forces a hiatus instead of a diphthong. *Dí-a* (day) looks like it should
glide (*i* + *a*), but the accent on the *í* pulls it into its own
syllable — that's why *día* is two syllables, not one. This is the same
accent system from [[A1.1.1.4 Accent Marks for Stress and Meaning]], just
applied to vowel pairs instead of consonant endings.

Getting this right matters for counting syllables correctly, which is the
first step in applying the natural stress rules.

## Key Points / Conjugation / Rules

- Strong vowels: *a*, *e*, *o*. Weak vowels: *i*, *u*.
- Strong + weak, weak + strong, or weak + weak → diphthong (one syllable).
- Strong + strong → hiatus (two syllables).
- A written accent on the weak vowel of what would otherwise be a diphthong
  forces a hiatus instead — *dí-a*, *ma-íz*, *ba-úl*.
- Diphthongs and hiatuses only involve vowels that are actually adjacent in
  the same word — a consonant in between means there's no vowel pair at
  all.

## Examples

| Spanish | English | Notes |
| ------- | ------- | ----- |
| bien | well/fine | *ie* = weak + strong → diphthong, one syllable. |
| cielo | sky | *ie* = weak + strong → diphthong: *cie-lo*. |
| cuidado | care/caution | *ui* = weak + weak → diphthong: *cui-da-do*. |
| ca-os | chaos | *ao* = strong + strong → hiatus, two syllables. |
| fe-o | ugly | *eo* = strong + strong → hiatus: *fe-o*. |
| dí-a | day | *ía* would normally be a diphthong, but the accent on *í* forces a hiatus: *dí-a*. |
| pa-ís | country | Same pattern — accent on *í* forces the hiatus: *pa-ís*. |

## Common Mistakes

- Treating any two adjacent vowels as automatically two syllables — most
  vowel pairs in Spanish actually glide together as a diphthong.
- Missing that an accent on a weak vowel (*í*, *ú*) next to a strong vowel
  breaks the diphthong into a hiatus — this is a common source of
  syllable-counting errors.
- Confusing this accent (which forces a hiatus) with the stress-marking
  accent from [[A1.1.1.4 Accent Marks for Stress and Meaning]] — it's the
  same written mark, but here its job is to separate the vowels, not just
  mark stress.
- Forgetting that weak + weak (*ui*, *iu*) still counts as a diphthong, not
  a hiatus — both vowels are weak, but they still glide together.

## Explanation

Beyond individual word stress (see [[A1.1.2.1 Natural Stress Rules]]),
Spanish sentences carry their own rise and fall in pitch — and that melody
is one of the clearest signals of whether you're making a statement or
asking a question, sometimes with no other change in the words at all.

**Statements** in Spanish typically end with a falling pitch. The voice
rises gently through the sentence and drops on the last stressed syllable —
a sense of closure, like putting a period down. *Vamos al cine* said as a
statement ends low and settled.

**Yes/no questions** — the kind answered with *sí* or *no* — usually end
with a rising pitch. The exact same words, *¿Vamos al cine?*, said with a
rise at the end turn a plain statement into a question, even though nothing
in the spelling changes except the added question marks. This is why
Spanish speakers rely on intonation so heavily for yes/no questions: word
order often doesn't change the way it does in English.

**Information questions** — the kind that start with a question word like
*qué*, *dónde*, *cuándo*, *cómo*, or *por qué* — behave differently. The
pitch rises on the question word near the start, then falls again by the
end, similar to a statement's ending. *¿Dónde vives?* rises early, on
*dónde*, then comes back down — it doesn't keep climbing to the end the way
a yes/no question does.

Getting the pitch pattern right matters as much as getting the words right:
a flat or wrongly-shaped intonation can make a question sound like a
statement, or make a speaker sound uncertain or abrupt even when the
grammar is perfect.

## Key Points / Conjugation / Rules

- Statements: pitch rises through the sentence, then falls on the final
  stressed syllable.
- Yes/no questions: pitch rises toward the end of the sentence.
- Information questions (with *qué*, *dónde*, *cuándo*, *cómo*, *por qué*,
  *quién*, *cuál*): pitch rises early, on the question word, then falls
  again by the end — closer to a statement's shape.
- The exact same word order can be a statement or a yes/no question in
  spoken Spanish — intonation is often the only difference the listener
  hears.
- Written Spanish marks this with ¿ and ? around a question — but in speech,
  it's the pitch pattern that actually signals the difference.

## Examples

| Spanish | English | Notes |
| ------- | ------- | ----- |
| Vamos al cine. | We're going to the movies. | Statement — pitch falls at the end. |
| ¿Vamos al cine? | Are we going to the movies? | Yes/no question — same words, pitch rises at the end. |
| Tienes hambre. | You're hungry. | Statement — falling pitch. |
| ¿Tienes hambre? | Are you hungry? | Yes/no question — rising pitch, same words. |
| ¿Dónde vives? | Where do you live? | Information question — pitch rises on *dónde*, falls by the end. |
| ¿Cuándo llega tu hermana? | When does your sister arrive? | Information question — rise on *cuándo*, fall by the end. |
| ¿Por qué estás triste? | Why are you sad? | Information question — rise on *por qué*, fall by the end. |

## Common Mistakes

- Using flat, unchanging pitch on a yes/no question, which can make it sound
  like a statement to a native listener.
- Letting the pitch keep rising all the way through an information question,
  copying the yes/no pattern instead of falling back down after the
  question word.
- Relying only on the written ¿ ? marks and ignoring pitch when speaking —
  in conversation there's no punctuation to see, only the melody.
- Over-exaggerating the rise on a yes/no question until it sounds like
  surprise or disbelief rather than a neutral question.

## Explanation

In [[A1.2.1.1 Standard Gender Endings]] you learned the strong pattern:
*-o* words tend to be masculine, *-a* words tend to be feminine. That
pattern holds most of the time — but Spanish has a real, well-known set of
exceptions that break it, and you'll run into them constantly, so they're
worth learning by name rather than being surprised by them one at a time.

The most common group is words ending in *-ma* that come from Greek and are
masculine despite ending in *-a*: *el problema* (the problem), *el mapa*
(the map), *el día* (the day), *el sistema* (the system), *el programa*
(the program). These aren't rare, obscure words — they're everyday
vocabulary, so it's worth memorizing this group as a set.

The reverse also happens: a handful of very common feminine words end in
*-o*, most notably *la mano* (the hand) and *la foto* (short for *la
fotografía*, the photo) and *la moto* (short for *la motocicleta*, the
motorcycle). Notice that *foto* and *moto* are feminine because they're
shortened forms of longer feminine words — the shortened form keeps the
original gender even though it now ends in *-o*.

A separate, trickier group is feminine nouns that start with a stressed
*a-* or *ha-* sound, like *el agua* (the water) and *el hacha* (the axe).
These nouns are grammatically feminine — you'd say *la* with any other
article or adjective — but in the singular they take *el* instead of *la*
purely to avoid two *a* sounds colliding (*la agua* is awkward to say). The
plural switches back to normal: *las aguas*. This is a pronunciation fix,
not a gender change, so don't let the *el* fool you into treating these as
masculine.

## Key Points / Conjugation / Rules

- *-ma* words from Greek are masculine despite ending in *-a*: *el
  problema, el mapa, el día, el sistema, el programa*.
- A few common *-o* words are feminine: *la mano, la foto, la moto*.
- Feminine nouns starting with a stressed *a-*/*ha-* sound take *el* in the
  singular for pronunciation only — they're still feminine, and adjectives
  describing them stay feminine: *el agua fría* (the cold water), not *el
  agua frío*.
- The plural of stressed *a-*/*ha-* nouns goes back to *las*: *las aguas,
  las hachas*.
- When in doubt about a noun that seems to break the pattern, check a
  dictionary rather than guessing from the ending alone.

## Examples

| Spanish | English | Notes |
| ------- | ------- | ----- |
| el problema | the problem | *-ma* ending, masculine despite *-a*. |
| el día | the day | Masculine despite *-a* ending. |
| la mano | the hand | Feminine despite *-o* ending. |
| la foto | the photo | Feminine — short for *la fotografía*. |
| el agua clara | the clear water | Feminine noun, *el* for pronunciation, feminine adjective *clara*. |
| las aguas del río | the waters of the river | Plural reverts to *las*. |
| el sistema nuevo | the new system | Masculine despite *-a*; adjective *nuevo* agrees as masculine. |

## Common Mistakes

- Treating *el problema* or *el día* as feminine because they end in *-a*.
- Treating *la mano* or *la foto* as masculine because they end in *-o*.
- Assuming *el agua* means *agua* is masculine, then wrongly pairing it
  with a masculine adjective like *el agua frío* instead of *el agua fría*.
- Forgetting to switch back to *las* in the plural for words like *agua*
  and *hacha*.

## Explanation

Every noun in Spanish has a gender — masculine or feminine — even for
objects that have nothing to do with sex, like a table or a book. This
isn't optional or a matter of opinion; it's baked into the word itself, and
it controls which articles (*el/la*) and adjectives you'll pair with that
noun later. The good news: most of the time, the last letter of the word
tells you which gender it is.

The core pattern is simple. Nouns ending in *-o* are usually masculine:
*el libro* (the book), *el zapato* (the shoe). Nouns ending in *-a* are
usually feminine: *la mesa* (the table), *la casa* (the house). That single
pattern covers a huge share of everyday Spanish nouns, which is why it's
worth locking in early — everything else you learn about gender builds on
top of it.

A few other endings are also reliable predictors, even though they're not
as famous as *-o/-a*. Words ending in *-ción, -sión, -dad,* or *-tad* are
almost always feminine: *la información, la ciudad, la libertad*. Words
ending in *-or* are usually masculine: *el color, el amor*. You don't need
to memorize every ending today — just notice that Spanish gives you strong
hints, and *-o* and *-a* are the two to reach for first.

Because gender determines the article and any adjective that describes the
noun, getting it right from the start saves you from having to relearn a
word later. When you meet a new noun, get in the habit of learning it with
its article — *el libro*, not just *libro* — so the gender sticks
automatically.

## Key Points / Conjugation / Rules

- Nouns ending in *-o* are usually masculine: *el libro, el carro, el
  zapato*.
- Nouns ending in *-a* are usually feminine: *la mesa, la casa, la
  ventana*.
- Nouns ending in *-ción, -sión, -dad,* or *-tad* are almost always
  feminine: *la nación, la decisión, la ciudad, la libertad*.
- Nouns ending in *-or* are usually masculine: *el color, el amor, el
  motor*.
- Always learn a new noun together with its article (*el* or *la*) rather
  than the bare word — that's the habit that makes gender automatic.

## Examples

| Spanish | English | Notes |
| ------- | ------- | ----- |
| el libro | the book | *-o* ending → masculine. |
| la mesa | the table | *-a* ending → feminine. |
| el zapato | the shoe | *-o* ending → masculine. |
| la ventana | the window | *-a* ending → feminine. |
| la ciudad | the city | *-dad* ending → feminine. |
| el color | the color | *-or* ending → masculine. |
| la información | the information | *-ción* ending → feminine. |

## Common Mistakes

- Assuming every noun ending in *-a* is feminine or every noun ending in
  *-o* is masculine without exception — the pattern is strong but not
  universal (see [[A1.2.1.2 Common Gender Exceptions]]).
- Learning a noun without its article, which makes the gender easy to
  forget or guess wrong later.
- Mixing up *-sión/-ción* (feminine) with *-or* (masculine) when a word has
  a similar sound to English, since English doesn't mark gender at all.
- Forgetting that gender affects the adjectives that describe the noun, not
  just the article in front of it.

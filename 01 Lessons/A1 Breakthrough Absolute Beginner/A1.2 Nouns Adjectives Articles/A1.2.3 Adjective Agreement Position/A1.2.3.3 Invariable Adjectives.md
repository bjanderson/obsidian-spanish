## Explanation

[[A1.2.3.1 Gender and Number Agreement]] showed you two regular patterns:
*-o*-ending adjectives change for both gender and number (*rojo, roja,
rojos, rojas*), while consonant- and *-e*-ending adjectives like *verde,
azul,* and *grande* only change for number, keeping one form for masculine
and feminine (*el carro azul, la casa azul, los carros azules*). This
lesson covers a smaller, extra-stubborn group: adjectives that don't
change at all, not even for number.

This fully invariable group shows up mostly among color words that are
actually borrowed nouns rather than true adjectives. *Naranja* (orange),
*rosa* (pink), *violeta* (violet), and *café* (brown) all work this way:
*una camisa naranja* (an orange shirt), *unas camisas naranja* (some orange
shirts) — no *-s* added, even in the plural. Grammatically, these words are
acting like "a shirt of orange," treating the color as a fixed label rather
than a true describing word, which is why they resist agreement entirely.

A very common, always-safe workaround is the *color* + [color noun]
construction, which sidesteps agreement altogether regardless of which
color you're using: *zapatos color café* (brown shoes), *una camisa color
salmón* (a salmon-colored shirt). Because *color* itself never changes and
the color word after it stays fixed, this pattern is a safe default any
time you're unsure whether a given color is invariable.

One more piece worth knowing: *marrón*, a regular adjective for brown that
does follow normal agreement rules (*marrón, marrones*), competes with
*café* across Latin America. In everyday neutral Latin American speech,
*café* is the more common, safe choice for "brown," with *color café* as
an even safer fallback when in doubt.

## Key Points / Conjugation / Rules

- Regular adjectives ending in a consonant or *-e* (like *verde, azul,
  grande*) change only for number, not gender — one review, per
  [[A1.2.3.1 Gender and Number Agreement]].
- A smaller set of color words borrowed from nouns — *naranja, rosa,
  violeta, café* — don't change for gender or number at all.
- The *color* + [color noun] construction always sidesteps agreement:
  *zapatos color café*.
- *Marrón* is a regular, fully agreeing adjective for brown; *café* is
  the common invariable alternative in neutral Latin American Spanish.
- *Claro* (light) and *oscuro* (dark) added after a color are themselves
  invariable: *verde claro, azul oscuro*.

## Examples

| Spanish | English | Notes |
| ------- | ------- | ----- |
| una camisa naranja | an orange shirt | Invariable — no *-a* added despite feminine noun. |
| unas camisas naranja | some orange shirts | Still no *-s* — fully invariable. |
| los zapatos café | the brown shoes | *café* stays the same in the plural. |
| una casa color rosa | a pink house | *color* construction, always safe. |
| las paredes verde claro | the light green walls | *claro* stays invariable after the color. |
| el carro azul oscuro | the dark blue car | *oscuro* stays invariable after the color. |

## Common Mistakes

- Adding *-s* to a fully invariable color word in the plural: writing
  *camisas naranjas* instead of *camisas naranja*.
- Confusing regular number-only adjectives like *verde* and *azul* (which
  do add *-s* in the plural) with the smaller, fully invariable group like
  *naranja* and *café* (which never change).
- Trying to make *claro* or *oscuro* agree with the noun, instead of
  leaving them fixed after the color they modify.
- Assuming *marrón* is the only word for brown — in neutral Latin American
  speech, *café* is at least as common and often the safer default.

## Explanation

In [[A1.2.2.2 Indefinite Articles]] you learned *un/una/unos/unas* for "a"
and "some." Now for the flip side: there are specific, common situations
where Spanish leaves the article out entirely, even though the English
translation includes "a." Learning these as a set now will save you from
sounding oddly formal or overly specific later.

The clearest case is stating someone's profession, nationality, or
religion right after *ser*, with no adjective describing it: *Soy
profesor* ("I'm a teacher"), *Es mexicana* ("She's Mexican"), *Es católico*
("He's Catholic"). Spanish treats the profession or category here almost
like an adjective describing the person, not a separate thing being
introduced — and adjectives don't take an article. The moment you add a
describing word, though, the article comes back: *Soy un profesor
excelente* ("I'm an excellent teacher") needs *un* because now you're
picking one excellent teacher out of a group, not just naming a category.

A second common case is after certain quantity words and prepositions like
*sin* (without) and *con* (with) when the noun is unspecified: *Vengo sin
paraguas* ("I'm coming without an umbrella"), *¿Tienes carro?* ("Do you
have a car?") in casual speech often drops the article too, especially in
yes/no questions about simply possessing something. This isn't a hard rule
the way the profession case is — it's more a tendency toward brevity when
the exact identity of the thing doesn't matter, only whether you have it
or not.

The through-line in both cases: Spanish uses the article when it's
identifying or singling out a specific instance of something, and skips it
when the noun is functioning more like a category or a description than a
specific object.

## Key Points / Conjugation / Rules

- No article after *ser* with an unmodified profession, nationality, or
  religion: *Soy profesor, Es mexicana, Es católico*.
- The article returns as soon as an adjective describes the noun: *Soy un
  profesor excelente*.
- The article is often dropped after *sin* and in simple possession
  questions: *sin paraguas, ¿Tienes carro?*.
- This omission pattern applies to indefinite articles (*un/una*) — the
  definite article rules from [[A1.2.2.1 Definite Articles]] still apply
  normally elsewhere.

## Examples

| Spanish | English | Notes |
| ------- | ------- | ----- |
| Soy enfermera. | I'm a nurse. | No article — unmodified profession after *ser*. |
| Soy una enfermera muy dedicada. | I'm a very dedicated nurse. | Article returns because an adjective describes her. |
| Es ingeniero. | He's an engineer. | No article — unmodified profession. |
| Salimos sin paraguas. | We left without an umbrella. | No article after *sin*. |
| ¿Tienes carro? | Do you have a car? | Casual possession question, article dropped. |
| Es una doctora excelente. | She's an excellent doctor. | Article returns with the adjective *excelente*. |

## Common Mistakes

- Adding *un/una* to an unmodified profession after *ser*: saying *Soy un
  profesor* when *Soy profesor* is correct.
- Forgetting the article once an adjective is added: saying *Soy profesor
  excelente* instead of *Soy un profesor excelente*.
- Over-applying this rule outside of *ser* + profession/nationality/
  religion, and dropping articles where they're actually needed.
- Assuming the *sin*/possession-question omission is a strict rule rather
  than a common, informal tendency.

## Explanation

[[A1.4.1.1 Subject Pronouns Overview]] covered that _tú_ is informal and _usted_
is formal. This lesson goes a level deeper: the actual social judgment calls
Spanish speakers make in real situations, since "informal vs. formal" alone
doesn't tell you what to do at the counter of a new pharmacy or when meeting a
friend's parent.

A useful starting rule: default to _usted_ with anyone you don't know yet — an
older person, someone in a position of authority (a boss, an official, a
doctor), or in any professional or service interaction (asking a stranger for
directions, talking to a store clerk). Default to _tú_ with people your own age
or younger that you're meeting in a casual context, and with anyone already
established as a friend, classmate, or family member.

The switch from _usted_ to _tú_ usually happens naturally as a relationship
becomes closer — a coworker you start seeing socially, a neighbor you get to
know. It's common, and not rude, to explicitly ask: _¿Nos podemos tutear?_
("Can we use _tú_ with each other?" — from _tutear_, "to address as _tú_").
Waiting for the other person to make the first move, or asking outright, avoids
the awkwardness of switching too soon.

Regional habits vary — some countries and communities lean more informal overall
(using _tú_ sooner, with more people) and others hold onto _usted_ longer, even
between family members in some regions. When in doubt in an unfamiliar context,
erring toward _usted_ is the safer choice: it can come across as slightly
distant if unnecessary, but it's never disrespectful, while an unwanted _tú_
can land as presumptuous.

## Key Points / Conjugation / Rules

- Default to _usted_: strangers, older people, authority figures, and
  professional/service contexts.
- Default to _tú_: peers your own age in casual settings, friends, classmates,
  family.
- _Tutear_ = to address someone as _tú_. _¿Nos podemos tutear?_ is a normal,
  polite way to propose switching from _usted_ to _tú_.
- Relationships often shift from _usted_ to _tú_ as they become closer — it's a
  one-directional move, not something you'd switch back from.
- When genuinely unsure, _usted_ is the safer default — it risks sounding a
  little distant, never disrespectful.

## Examples

| Spanish                                     | English                                     | Notes                              |
| -------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------- |
| Buenas tardes, ¿me puede ayudar?                   | Good afternoon, can you help me?                   | *usted*, addressing a store clerk         |
| Oye, ¿tienes un minuto?                            | Hey, do you have a minute?                          | *tú*, addressing a friend                 |
| Mucho gusto, doctor. ¿Cómo está usted?             | Nice to meet you, doctor. How are you?             | *usted*, professional/authority context   |
| Ya nos conocemos bien, ¿nos podemos tutear?         | We know each other well now, can we use tú?        | proposing the switch to *tú*              |
| Perdone, señora, ¿sabe dónde queda el banco?        | Excuse me, ma'am, do you know where the bank is?   | *usted*, addressing a stranger            |
| ¿Tú eres el hermano de Ana?                        | Are you Ana's brother?                              | *tú*, casual peer-to-peer context         |

## Common Mistakes

- Using _tú_ immediately with an older stranger or an authority figure — it can
  come across as overly familiar or even disrespectful in that context.
- Assuming the register is fixed across all of Latin America — some regions lean
  more informal and others more formal, so watch and match local usage rather
  than applying one rule everywhere.
- Switching to _tú_ without any signal from the other person — waiting for them
  to initiate, or asking directly, is the safer path.

## Explanation

Short-form possessives are the everyday words for "my, your, his, her, our, their" —
the ones that sit right before a noun, like _mi casa_ ("my house") or _tu libro_
("your book"). They're called "short form" because Spanish also has a longer set of
possessives (_mío, tuyo..._) that you'll meet later; for now, this lesson covers only
the short, everyday kind.

Here's the part that's different from English: these possessives agree with the
thing being owned, not with the owner. _Mis llaves_ ("my keys") takes the plural _mis_
because _llaves_ is plural — it has nothing to do with how many people own the keys.
Most of these words only change for number (singular/plural), but _nuestro_ also
changes for gender, matching _-o/-a_ the same way regular adjectives do.

One more piece worth knowing early: _su/sus_ can mean "his," "her," "your (formal),"
or "their" — Spanish doesn't distinguish between them with the possessive itself.
When it's not clear from context whose thing you're talking about, add _de él_,
_de ella_, _de usted_, or _de ellos_ to clarify: _el carro de ella_ ("her car").

## Key Points / Conjugation / Rules

| Meaning                          | Singular                     | Plural                           |
| -------------------------------- | ---------------------------- | -------------------------------- |
| my                               | mi perro                     | mis perros                       |
| your (tú)                        | tu casa                      | tus casas                        |
| his / her / your (usted) / their | su amigo                     | sus amigos                       |
| our                              | nuestro carro / nuestra casa | nuestros carros / nuestras casas |

- Possessives agree in number with the noun they modify; _nuestro/nuestra_ also
  agrees in gender.
- _Su/sus_ is ambiguous — clarify with _de él, de ella, de usted,_ or _de ellos_
  when the owner isn't obvious from context.
- Spanish normally skips the possessive with body parts and clothing when the owner
  is clear from context — use the definite article instead: _Me duele el codo_ ("My
  elbow hurts"), not _Me duele mi codo_.

## Examples

| Spanish                          | English                   | Notes                           |
| -------------------------------- | ------------------------- | ------------------------------- |
| Mi hermano vive en Lima.         | My brother lives in Lima. | _mi_ + singular noun            |
| ¿Dónde están tus llaves?         | Where are your keys?      | _tus_ + plural noun             |
| Su casa es muy grande.           | Her house is very big.    | _su_, ambiguous owner           |
| Es la casa de ella, no de él.    | It's her house, not his.  | clarifying with _de ella/de él_ |
| Nuestro carro está en el taller. | Our car is at the shop.   | _nuestro_, masculine            |
| Nuestra familia es grande.       | Our family is big.        | _nuestra_, feminine             |

## Common Mistakes

- Matching the possessive to the owner's gender instead of the noun's — _su libro_
  stays _su_ regardless of whether the owner is male or female.
- Forgetting that _nuestro_ changes for gender while _mi, tu,_ and _su_ don't:
  _nuestra casa_, not _nuestro casa_.
- Adding a possessive to a body part when context already makes the owner obvious —
  _Me lavo las manos_ ("I wash my hands"), not _Me lavo mis manos_.

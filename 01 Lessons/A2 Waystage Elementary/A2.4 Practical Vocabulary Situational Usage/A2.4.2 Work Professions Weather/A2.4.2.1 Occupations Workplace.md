## Explanation

"What do you do for work?" is one of the first questions that comes up when meeting
someone new. This lesson covers common occupations, plus the vocabulary you need to
talk about where you work and what a workday looks like.

## Key Points / Conjugation / Rules

**Common occupations:**

| Spanish                    | English            |
| ----------------------------- | ---------------------- |
| el/la doctor(a)               | doctor                  |
| el/la maestro(a)               | teacher                 |
| el/la ingeniero(a)             | engineer                |
| el/la abogado(a)               | lawyer                  |
| el/la enfermero(a)             | nurse                   |
| el/la cocinero(a)              | cook, chef               |
| el/la mesero(a)                | waiter/waitress          |
| el policía / la mujer policía  | police officer           |
| el/la contador(a)              | accountant               |
| el/la programador(a)           | programmer               |

**Workplace vocabulary:**

| Spanish             | English            |
| ---------------------- | ---------------------- |
| la oficina              | office                  |
| el trabajo / el empleo  | job/work                |
| el/la jefe(a)           | boss                    |
| el/la compañero(a) de trabajo | coworker         |
| el sueldo               | salary                  |
| el horario              | schedule                |
| la reunión              | meeting                 |
| renunciar               | to quit                 |
| contratar               | to hire                 |

- Most occupation nouns change ending for gender, just like other nouns: *-o* → *-a*
  (*maestro/maestra*), or add *-a* to a consonant ending (*doctor/doctora*).
  A few stay the same for both genders — *el/la policía*, *el/la estudiante*.
- Use *ser* + occupation to say what someone does for a living, and normally drop the
  article: *Soy maestra* (I'm a teacher), not *Soy una maestra* — the article is
  dropped for professions unless you add a descriptive adjective (*Soy una maestra
  excelente*).
- *Trabajar de/como* + occupation is another common pattern: *Trabajo de mesero*
  (I work as a waiter).
- *Trabajo* and *empleo* both mean "job," but *trabajo* is broader (can also mean
  "work" as an activity), while *empleo* specifically refers to formal employment.

## Examples

| Spanish                                    | English                              | Notes                    |
| --------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------ |
| Mi papá es ingeniero.                         | My dad is an engineer.                       | *ser*, no article               |
| Ella trabaja de enfermera en el hospital.     | She works as a nurse at the hospital.        | *trabajar de*                  |
| Tengo una reunión con mi jefe a las diez.     | I have a meeting with my boss at ten.         | *jefe*, personal *a* not needed |
| Mi compañera de trabajo renunció ayer.        | My coworker quit yesterday.                  | *renunciar*, past               |
| Buscan contratar a un programador nuevo.      | They're looking to hire a new programmer.     | *contratar a*, personal *a*     |
| ¿Cuál es tu horario esta semana?              | What's your schedule this week?              | *horario*, question            |

## Common Mistakes

- Using an article with *ser* + occupation: *Soy un abogado* — while not always
  "wrong," the more natural, standard form drops the article: *Soy abogado*.
  Include the article only when the profession has a descriptive adjective attached.
- Forgetting the personal *a* before a specific-person direct object involving a job
  action: *Contratamos el nuevo programador* is wrong — it needs *Contratamos al nuevo
  programador*.
- Mixing up *trabajo* (job/work, general) and *empleo* (formal employment) — they
  overlap but aren't always interchangeable, especially in official or written
  contexts (*solicitud de empleo* = job application, not *solicitud de trabajo*).
- Assuming every occupation noun changes for gender the regular way — *el/la policía*
  and *el/la estudiante* don't change form, only the article does.

## Explanation

Getting around a Spanish-speaking city or country means knowing your transportation
options and how to buy a ticket for them. This lesson covers the main modes of
transit and the vocabulary you need at a ticket counter, bus stop, or train station.

## Key Points / Conjugation / Rules

**Modes of transit:**

| Spanish                  | English            |
| ---------------------------- | ---------------------- |
| el autobús / el camión (Mex.) | bus                     |
| el metro                     | subway                  |
| el tren                      | train                   |
| el taxi                      | taxi                    |
| el avión                     | airplane                |
| el barco                     | boat                    |
| la bicicleta                 | bicycle                 |
| a pie                        | on foot                 |

**Buying tickets:**

| Spanish                    | English            |
| ------------------------------- | ---------------------- |
| el boleto                        | ticket                  |
| la taquilla / la ventanilla      | ticket window            |
| el pasaje                        | fare, ticket             |
| ida y vuelta                     | round trip               |
| solo de ida                      | one way                  |
| la parada                        | stop (bus/train)          |
| el andén                         | platform                 |
| abordar                          | to board                 |

- *Boleto* is the standard word for "ticket" across Latin America. In Spain, *billete*
  is used instead — but be careful: in Latin America, *billete* usually means a
  currency bill/banknote, not a transit ticket, so stick with *boleto* here.
- Use *tomar* or *coger* to say "take" a form of transit: *tomar el autobús* (to take
  the bus). *Coger* is common in most of Latin America but can carry a vulgar meaning
  in some countries (Mexico, Argentina), so *tomar* is the safer default choice.
- *El camión* means "truck" in most Spanish-speaking countries, but in Mexico it
  commonly means "bus" — worth knowing so you're not confused by the different sense.
- Use *ida y vuelta* for round trip and *solo de ida* for one-way, when buying
  tickets.

## Examples

| Spanish                                    | English                              | Notes                    |
| --------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------ |
| ¿A qué hora sale el próximo autobús?          | What time does the next bus leave?           | *autobús*, question             |
| Quiero un boleto de ida y vuelta a Cancún.    | I want a round-trip ticket to Cancún.        | *boleto*, *ida y vuelta*        |
| ¿Dónde queda la taquilla?                     | Where's the ticket window?                    | *taquilla*, location            |
| Tomamos el metro para llegar más rápido.      | We took the subway to get there faster.       | *tomar el metro*, past          |
| El tren sale del andén número tres.           | The train leaves from platform three.          | *andén*, *del*                  |
| ¿Cuánto cuesta el pasaje a la ciudad?         | How much does the fare to the city cost?      | *pasaje*, question              |

## Common Mistakes

- Using *coger* freely across all countries — it's the everyday word for "to take" in
  Spain and some Latin American countries, but has a vulgar meaning in Mexico and
  Argentina; *tomar* works safely everywhere.
- Confusing *el camión* (truck, or "bus" in Mexico specifically) with *el carro/coche*
  (car) — context and country matter here.
- Forgetting *de* before a destination in ticket phrases: *un boleto Cancún* is
  incomplete — it needs *un boleto a Cancún* or *un boleto para Cancún*.
- Using *billete* to mean "ticket" in Latin American Spanish — it's the Spain term for
  a transit ticket, but in Latin America it usually means a banknote. Use *boleto*
  for "ticket" instead.

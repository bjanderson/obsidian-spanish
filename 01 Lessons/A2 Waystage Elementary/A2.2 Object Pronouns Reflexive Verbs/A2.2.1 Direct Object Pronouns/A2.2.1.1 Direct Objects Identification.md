## Explanation

A direct object is the thing or person that directly receives the action of
the verb — the "what" or "who" the verb is being done to. In *Veo la
película* ("I watch the movie"), *la película* is the direct object: it's the
thing being watched.

Once something has already been mentioned, you don't have to keep repeating
the full noun — Spanish lets you swap it out for a short pronoun instead,
just like English swaps "the movie" for "it." *¿Ves la película? Sí, la veo*
("Do you see the movie? Yes, I see it") — *la* stands in for *la película*.

Here are the direct object pronouns:

| Pronoun | Meaning                          |
| -------- | ----------------------------------- |
| me       | me                                    |
| te       | you (informal)                        |
| lo       | him / it (masculine)                  |
| la       | her / it (feminine)                   |
| nos      | us                                     |
| los      | them (masc./mixed), you all (formal)  |
| las      | them (fem.), you all (formal, all women) |

Two details matter when identifying a direct object. First, gender and
number: the pronoun has to match the noun it's replacing — *lo* for a
masculine singular thing, *las* for a feminine plural group. Second, when the
direct object is a specific person, the personal *a* shows up in the full
sentence (*Conozco a Juan*), even though the pronoun itself (*lo conozco*)
doesn't carry that *a* — the *a* was only ever there to mark the noun, not the
pronoun.

## Key Points / Conjugation / Rules

- A direct object answers "what?" or "whom?" after the verb — it's the thing
  or person directly acted upon.
- Direct object pronouns must agree in gender and number with the noun they
  replace: *el libro → lo*, *la mesa → la*, *los libros → los*, *las mesas →
  las*.
- *Me*, *te*, and *nos* don't change for gender — they work the same
  regardless of who or what they refer to.
- A specific-person direct object takes the personal *a* in its full-noun
  form (*Veo a María*), but the pronoun that replaces it doesn't add an *a*
  (*La veo*).
- *Lo* also has a special "neuter" use, referring to an idea or fact rather
  than a specific noun: *No lo sé* ("I don't know [that]").

## Examples

| Spanish                         | English                        | Notes                        |
| ---------------------------------- | ----------------------------------- | -------------------------------- |
| ¿Conoces a Juan? — No lo conozco.  | Do you know Juan? — I don't know him. | *lo* replaces *a Juan*, masculine person |
| ¿Tienes las llaves? — Sí, las tengo. | Do you have the keys? — Yes, I have them. | *las* replaces *las llaves*, feminine plural |
| ¿Ves a las mujeres? — Sí, las veo. | Do you see the women? — Yes, I see them. | *las* replaces *a las mujeres*, feminine plural |
| ¿Quieres ver la película? — Sí, quiero verla. | Do you want to watch the movie? — Yes, I want to watch it. | *la* replaces *la película* |
| ¿Qué dijo ella? — No lo entendí.   | What did she say? — I didn't understand it. | neuter *lo*, refers to an idea |
| Me llamas después, ¿verdad?        | You'll call me later, right?        | *me* as direct object            |

## Common Mistakes

- Forgetting to match gender/number — using *lo* for a feminine noun like *la
  mesa* instead of *la*.
- Adding a personal *a* to the pronoun itself — *a lo veo* is wrong; the *a*
  only appears with the full noun (*veo a Juan*), never with the pronoun.
- Confusing the neuter *lo* (referring to an idea) with the masculine *lo*
  (replacing a specific masculine noun) — context makes clear which is meant.

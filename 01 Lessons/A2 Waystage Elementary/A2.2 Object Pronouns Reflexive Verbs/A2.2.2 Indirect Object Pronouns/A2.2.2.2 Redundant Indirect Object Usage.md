## Explanation

Here's something that trips up English speakers constantly: with a long list
of common verbs, Spanish keeps the indirect object pronoun (*le*/*les*) even
when the recipient is already named. *Le envié una carta a María* literally
piles up "to her" and "to María" in the same sentence — and that's not a
mistake, it's just how Spanish works.

English never does this. Nobody says "I sent her a letter to Mary" — English
picks one or the other. But Spanish expects both at the same time with many
high-frequency verbs: *dar* (to give), *decir* (to tell), *contar* (to tell a
story), *comprar* (to buy for someone), *escribir* (to write to someone),
*prestar* (to lend), *devolver* (to return something to someone), and more.

The good news is you can always drop the "*a* + person" part and just keep
the pronoun — *Le envié una carta* still makes perfect sense on its own,
since *le* alone tells you someone received the letter. What you can't do is
the reverse: dropping *le* and keeping only *a María* sounds off to a native
speaker, even though it seems like it should be enough information.

This isn't every verb in Spanish — it's specifically this group of
give/tell/communicate verbs. But because they're so common, getting used to
the redundant pronoun is one of the fastest ways to make your Spanish sound
natural.

## Key Points / Conjugation / Rules

| Pattern                                   | Example                                       |
| -------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------- |
| Pronoun + verb + "a" + person (full form)     | *Le di el libro a Juan.*                            |
| Pronoun + verb (recipient dropped, still fine) | *Le di el libro.*                                  |
| ~~Verb + "a" + person only (pronoun dropped)~~ | *Di el libro a Juan.* — sounds incomplete/incorrect |

- *Le* and *les* stay in the sentence even when *a + [person]* is also
  stated — this is expected, not optional.
- Common verbs that take this redundant pattern: *dar, decir, contar,
  comprar, escribir, enviar/mandar, pedir, prestar, devolver, quitar, robar*.
- You can drop the "*a* + person" phrase and keep just the pronoun — the
  reverse (dropping the pronoun, keeping only "*a* + person") is not
  standard.
- *Le*/*les* stays the same regardless of gender — it doesn't shift for "a
  Juan" vs. "a María."

## Examples

| Spanish                                | English                              | Notes                    |
| ----------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------ |
| Le di el libro a Juan.                    | I gave the book to Juan.                    | redundant *le* + named recipient |
| Le dije la verdad a María.                | I told María the truth.                     | redundant *le* + named recipient |
| Le compré un carro a mi esposa.           | I bought my wife a car.                     | redundant *le* + named recipient |
| Le presté la bicicleta a Luisa.           | I lent Luisa the bike.                      | redundant *le* + named recipient |
| ¿Le devolviste el libro a tu profesor?    | Did you return the book to your professor?  | redundant *le* + named recipient |
| Les mandé una carta a mis abuelos.        | I sent my grandparents a letter.            | redundant *les* + named recipients |

## Common Mistakes

- Dropping *le*/*les* when the recipient is already named — *Di el libro a
  Juan* sounds incomplete to a native speaker; it should be *Le di el libro a
  Juan*.
- Assuming this rule applies to every verb — it's specific to a group of
  give/tell/communicate verbs, not a universal rule for all indirect objects.
- Trying to make *le* agree with gender when the person is named — *le*
  stays *le* whether the recipient is *Juan* or *María*.

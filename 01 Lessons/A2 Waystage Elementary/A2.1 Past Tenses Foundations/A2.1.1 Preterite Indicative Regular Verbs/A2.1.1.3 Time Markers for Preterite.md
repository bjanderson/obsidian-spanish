## Explanation

Once you know the preterite endings, the next skill is knowing *when* to reach
for them. Certain time words point straight at the preterite because they name
a finished stretch of time — a day, a night, a week that's over and done. When
one of these words shows up in a sentence, it's a strong signal: the action
inside that time frame is complete, so the preterite is the right tense.

Think of *ayer* (yesterday) as the anchor. It names a day that's completely in
the past, so anything you did *ayer* gets the preterite: *Ayer trabajé mucho*
("Yesterday I worked a lot"). *Anoche* (last night) works the same way — it's a
specific, finished stretch of time, not an ongoing one.

Longer expressions like *la semana pasada* (last week) and *el mes pasado*
(last month) follow the same logic — the word *pasado/pasada* ("past," "last")
marks the time period as closed. And *hace* + a length of time (*hace dos
días*, "two days ago") measures backward from now to a specific finished
point.

These words aren't a rigid rule, but they're a reliable shortcut: see one of
them, and your first instinct should be to reach for the preterite.

## Key Points / Conjugation / Rules

| Time Marker          | English                | Notes                                    |
| ---------------------- | ------------------------- | -------------------------------------------- |
| ayer                    | yesterday                  | most common preterite marker                |
| anoche                  | last night                  | a specific, finished night                  |
| anteayer                | the day before yesterday    | two days back, still a closed day           |
| la semana pasada        | last week                   | *pasado/pasada* signals "closed period"     |
| el mes pasado           | last month                  | same *pasado* pattern                       |
| el año pasado           | last year                   | same *pasado* pattern                       |
| hace + [time]           | [time] ago                  | *hace dos horas* = "two hours ago"          |
| de repente              | suddenly                    | marks a sudden, completed event             |

- These markers name a closed, finished stretch of time — that's what pulls in
  the preterite instead of another past tense.
- *Pasado*/*pasada* agrees with the noun it modifies: *el mes pasado*, *la
  semana pasada*.
- *Hace* + time expression measures backward from the present moment to a
  specific point — *hace tres días* ("three days ago").

## Examples

| Spanish                                | English                             | Notes                    |
| ------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------- | ------------------------ |
| Ayer llamé a mi hermano.                    | Yesterday I called my brother.            | *ayer* + preterite        |
| Anoche no dormí bien.                       | Last night I didn't sleep well.           | *anoche* + preterite      |
| La semana pasada viajamos a Cusco.          | Last week we traveled to Cusco.           | *la semana pasada*        |
| Hace tres días perdí mi celular.            | Three days ago I lost my phone.           | *hace* + time             |
| El año pasado ella se graduó.               | Last year she graduated.                  | *el año pasado*           |
| De repente empezó a llover.                 | Suddenly it started to rain.              | *de repente*               |

## Common Mistakes

- Forgetting to make *pasado*/*pasada* agree with the noun — *la semana
  pasada*, not *la semana pasado*.
- Using *hace* with the imperfect out of habit — *hace* + a specific amount of
  time almost always pairs with the preterite, since it points at one finished
  moment.
- Confusing *anoche* ("last night") with *esta noche* ("tonight") — they refer
  to opposite ends of the day and take different tenses.

## Explanation

*Hay que* + infinitive expresses a general necessity — something that needs to be
done, without saying exactly who has to do it. It's the closest Spanish equivalent to
English "one has to..." or "it's necessary to..." Unlike *tener que*, which is tied
to a specific subject (*tengo*, *tienes*, *tiene*...), *hay que* never changes form.

## Key Points / Conjugation / Rules

- Formula: **hay que + infinitive** — always just *hay que*, no conjugation.
- *Hay* comes from the verb *haber*, the same *hay* used for "there is/there are."
- Because it's impersonal, *hay que* doesn't point at any specific person — it states
  a general rule, expectation, or necessity that applies broadly.
- Compare with *tener que*, which names who has the obligation: *Tengo que estudiar*
  (I have to study — specifically me) vs. *Hay que estudiar* (One has to study / You
  need to study — a general statement, not aimed at anyone in particular).

## Examples

| Spanish                                  | English                                  | Notes                              |
| -------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------- |
| Hay que estudiar para aprender.              | One has to study to learn.                   | general statement about learning    |
| Hay que llegar temprano a la reunión.        | You need to arrive early to the meeting.     | workplace norm, no specific subject |
| Para cocinar bien, hay que practicar mucho.  | To cook well, you have to practice a lot.    | general advice                      |
| Hay que pensar antes de hablar.              | You have to think before speaking.           | general life advice                 |
| Hay que respetar las reglas del lugar.       | One has to respect the rules of the place.   | impersonal social norm              |
| No hay que preocuparse tanto.                | There's no need to worry so much.            | negative impersonal statement       |

## Common Mistakes

- Trying to conjugate *hay*: *hayo que*, *hayes que* don't exist — *hay que* never
  changes, no matter who's involved.
- Using *hay que* when the obligation is actually specific to one person — in that
  case, *tener que* is the right choice (*Tengo que llamar a mi mamá*, not *Hay que
  llamar a mi mamá*, unless you really mean it as a general rule for anyone).
- Confusing this *hay* with the identical-looking *hay* for "there is/are" — context
  makes the difference: *Hay* + noun = "there is/are"; *hay que* + infinitive =
  "one has to."

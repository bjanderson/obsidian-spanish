## Explanation

In English, you can connect two ideas with "that," "who," or "which" — or
sometimes skip the connecting word entirely: "The book I read was good."
Spanish uses **relative pronouns** to do this same job of linking a
description back to a noun, and the good news is there's one word that
covers almost every case: **que**.

## Key Points / Conjugation / Rules

- **Que** (that, who, which) is the default, all-purpose relative pronoun.
  It works for **both people and things**: *El estudiante que ganó el
  concurso es muy talentoso* (The student who won the contest is very
  talented); *La casa que compraron está en el centro* (The house that they
  bought is downtown).
- Unlike English, **you can never skip the relative pronoun in Spanish**.
  English lets you say "The book I read," dropping "that" — Spanish always
  needs *que*: *El libro que leí* (never just *El libro leí*).
- *Que* is used in **restrictive clauses** — clauses that are essential to
  identifying which person or thing you mean, with no commas setting them
  off: *El libro que leí fue interesante* (The book that I read was
  interesting — specifies which book).
- When you need a relative pronoun after a **preposition**, or in a
  **non-restrictive clause** (extra information set off by commas), *que*
  usually isn't the right choice — those situations call for *quien* or
  *el que*, covered in the next lesson.
- As a rule of thumb: when in doubt, **que** is your safest default for
  connecting a description to a noun in everyday Spanish.

## Examples

| Spanish                                       | English                                | Notes                       |
| ---------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------- |
| El libro que leí fue interesante.                     | The book that I read was interesting.          | *que*, thing                        |
| La mujer que trabaja aquí es mi vecina.               | The woman who works here is my neighbor.        | *que*, person                         |
| El carro que compramos es muy cómodo.                 | The car that we bought is very comfortable.      | *que*, thing                            |
| Los estudiantes que estudian más aprueban.            | The students who study more pass.                | *que*, plural people                      |
| La película que vimos anoche fue excelente.           | The movie that we watched last night was excellent. | *que*, thing                                |
| El profesor que enseña matemáticas es muy paciente.   | The teacher who teaches math is very patient.      | *que*, person                                 |

## Common Mistakes

- Dropping *que* the way English drops "that": *El libro leí fue
  interesante* is wrong — Spanish always requires it: *El libro que leí
  fue interesante.*
- Using *quien* instead of *que* in a restrictive clause: *La mujer quien
  trabaja aquí* sounds wrong to native speakers — restrictive clauses call
  for *que*: *La mujer que trabaja aquí.*
- Adding commas around a restrictive clause: *El libro, que leí, fue
  interesante* changes the meaning — without commas, *que* identifies which
  book; with commas, it becomes extra (non-restrictive) information about a
  book already identified another way.
- Forgetting that *que* works for both people and things: some learners
  assume *que* is only for things because of its similarity to "that" —
  it's genuinely the default for people too.

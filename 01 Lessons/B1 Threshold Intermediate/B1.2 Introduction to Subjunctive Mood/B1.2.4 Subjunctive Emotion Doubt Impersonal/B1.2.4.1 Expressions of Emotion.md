## Explanation

You've now covered subjunctive triggers based on what you want ([[B1.2.3.1 Verbs of Desire and Will|desire]])
and what you're asking someone else to do ([[B1.2.3.2 Requests and Recommendations|requests]]).
This lesson covers a different kind of trigger: **emotional reactions**. Whenever
you react emotionally to something someone else does or something that happens —
happiness, fear, sadness, surprise — the subjunctive shows up in the clause
describing what caused that feeling.

This might feel surprising at first, because the thing you're reacting to is often
completely true and already happened. But Spanish isn't using the subjunctive here
to mark uncertainty — it's marking that the focus is on your *emotional reaction*,
not on confirming the fact itself.

## Key Points / Conjugation / Rules

- The pattern is the same shape you already know: **[emotional expression] + que
  + [verb in subjunctive]**.
- **Me alegra que** (it makes me happy that / I'm glad that) is one of the most
  common: *Me alegra que estés aquí* (I'm glad you're here).
- **Tengo miedo de que** (I'm afraid that) always keeps the *de* — don't drop it:
  *Tengo miedo de que llueva* (I'm afraid it will rain).
- Other common emotion triggers work the same way: **me sorprende que** (it
  surprises me that), **me pone triste que** (it makes me sad that), **siento
  que** (I'm sorry that / I feel that), **me molesta que** (it bothers me that).
- Even when the fact is 100% certain, the subjunctive still applies, because the
  trigger is the emotion, not the certainty of the fact: *Me alegra que hoy sea
  viernes* (I'm glad today is Friday) — there's zero doubt it's Friday, but the
  subjunctive still appears because the sentence is about your reaction to it.
- Many of these expressions include a little word that names whose emotion it
  is (*me, te, le, nos, les*) — the **indirect object pronoun**: *me alegra, te
  sorprende, le molesta*.

## Examples

| Spanish                                     | English                               | Notes                          |
| --------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------- | --------------------------------- |
| Me alegra que estés aquí.                     | I'm glad you're here.                     | *me alegra que*                     |
| Tengo miedo de que lleguemos tarde.           | I'm afraid we'll arrive late.               | *tener miedo de que*, *nosotros*      |
| Me sorprende que no hayas llamado.            | It surprises me that you haven't called.    | *me sorprende que*                    |
| Le molesta que fumen dentro de la casa.       | It bothers him/her that they smoke inside the house. | *le molesta que*, plural subject     |
| Siento mucho que hayas perdido tu trabajo.    | I'm very sorry that you lost your job.        | *sentir que*                            |
| Nos pone tristes que te vayas tan pronto.     | It makes us sad that you're leaving so soon.   | *nos pone tristes que*, *nosotros*      |

## Common Mistakes

- Dropping the *de* in *tener miedo de que*: *Tengo miedo que llueva* sounds
  incomplete to most speakers — keep the full *tener miedo de que*.
- Switching to the indicative because the fact seems certain: *Me alegra que
  hoy es viernes* is wrong — the emotional trigger forces subjunctive
  regardless of how certain the fact is.
- Forgetting the indirect object pronoun that names whose emotion it is:
  *Alegra que estés aquí* sounds unnatural without *me* — Spanish speakers
  say *Me alegra que estés aquí.*
- Confusing *sentir* (to feel/be sorry) as an emotion trigger with *sentir*
  meaning "to sense" physically — context makes the difference, but followed
  by *que* + subjunctive it almost always means "to feel sorry that."

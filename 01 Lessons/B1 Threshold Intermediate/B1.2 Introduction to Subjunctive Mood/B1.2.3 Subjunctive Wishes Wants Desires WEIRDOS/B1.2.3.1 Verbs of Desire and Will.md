## Explanation

You already know the subjunctive's basic shape: [[B1.2.1.2 Main Clause Que Subordinate Structure|main clause + que + subordinate clause]],
with the subordinate verb conjugated using the [[B1.2.2.1 Present Subjunctive Stems|stems]] you've
already learned. Now it's time to meet the verbs that actually *trigger* that
pattern in real conversation.

Verbs of desire and will are some of the most common subjunctive triggers you'll
hear. When you want, hope, or wish that someone else does something, Spanish
switches the second verb into the subjunctive — but only if the subject changes
between the two clauses. This lesson covers three of the most frequent ones:
**querer que** (to want that), **esperar que** (to hope that), and **desear que**
(to wish that).

## Key Points / Conjugation / Rules

- The pattern is always: **[trigger verb in indicative] + que + [verb in
  subjunctive]**. Example: *Quiero que* (I want) + *que* + *estudies* (subjunctive
  of *estudiar*).
- The subjunctive only appears when the **subject changes** between the two
  clauses. Compare:
  - *Quiero que estudies español.* (I want **you** to study Spanish — two
    different subjects, so the second verb is subjunctive.)
  - *Quiero estudiar español.* (I want **to study** Spanish — same subject for
    both verbs, so the second verb stays in the infinitive, with no *que* at
    all.)
- **Querer que** (to want that) is the most common of the three and works with
  any subject: *quiero que, quieres que, queremos que...*
- **Esperar que** (to hope that) expresses hope rather than a demand — it's
  softer than *querer que*, closer to "I hope" than "I want."
- **Desear que** (to wish that) is close in meaning to *esperar que* but a
  little more formal — you'll see it often in greeting-card language and
  polite wishes (*Deseo que tengas un buen día*), less often in casual
  everyday speech.
- These verbs are typically conjugated in the **first person** (*quiero,
  espero, deseo*) since you're usually expressing your own wishes about
  someone else's actions, but any subject can use them: *¿Quieres que te
  ayude?* (Do you want me to help you?)

## Examples

| Spanish                                    | English                              | Notes                          |
| ------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------- | -------------------------------- |
| Quiero que estudies más.                    | I want you to study more.               | *querer que*                       |
| Espero que te mejores pronto.               | I hope you get better soon.             | *esperar que*                      |
| Deseo que tengas un buen día.               | I wish you a good day.                  | *desear que*                       |
| ¿Quieres que te ayude con la tarea?         | Do you want me to help you with the homework? | *querer que*, question             |
| Esperamos que lleguen a tiempo.             | We hope they arrive on time.             | *esperar que*, *nosotros* subject    |
| Quiero estudiar más.                        | I want to study more.                    | same subject → infinitive, no *que*  |

## Common Mistakes

- Using the subjunctive when the subject doesn't change: *Quiero que estudie*
  is wrong if you mean "I want to study" — with one subject, it's *Quiero
  estudiar*, no *que* and no subjunctive.
- Forgetting *que* entirely when the subject does change: *Quiero estudies* is
  not valid Spanish — *que* is required to link the two clauses.
- Conjugating the second verb in the indicative instead of the subjunctive:
  *Espero que llegas a tiempo* should be *Espero que llegues a tiempo* — the
  trigger verb forces subjunctive in the subordinate clause.
- Treating *esperar* as always meaning "to wait for" — followed by *que* +
  subjunctive, it means "to hope that," a different sense from *esperar el
  autobús* (to wait for the bus).

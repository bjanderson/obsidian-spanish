## Explanation

Every verb you've conjugated so far — present, preterite, imperfect, the perfect
tenses — belongs to the **indicative mood**. Spanish has a second mood, the
**subjunctive**, that you switch into for a specific category of sentences: ones
about wishes, doubts, emotions, or things that aren't confirmed fact. This lesson
introduces the concept. The lessons after this one will teach the actual
conjugations and the specific patterns ("triggers") that call for it.

## Key Points / Conjugation / Rules

**The traditional definition:**

| Mood         | Used for                                      | Example |
| -------------- | ------------------------------------------------ | --------- |
| Indicative     | facts, certainty, objective reality                | *Ella escribe.* (She writes.) |
| Subjunctive    | subjectivity, doubt, desire, emotion, uncertainty  | *Espero que ella escriba.* (I hope she writes.) |

- Spanish also has a third mood, the **imperative**, used for direct commands (*¡Escribe!* — Write!) — not the focus of this lesson, but worth knowing it exists alongside these two.
- The subjunctive almost always shows up in a **subordinate clause** — a second clause
  attached to a main clause with *que* — not as a sentence's only verb. You'll rarely
  see subjunctive standing completely alone.
- Here's the honest problem with the textbook definition: it's true, but it doesn't
  actually help you decide, in the moment, whether a sentence needs it. *Tienes que
  lavar el carro antes de que llegue tu papá* (You have to wash the car before your
  dad arrives) uses the subjunctive *llegue* — but there's no real doubt that dad is
  coming. The definition alone doesn't predict that.
- The more reliable approach — and the one the rest of this section uses — is
  learning specific **subjunctive triggers**: fixed phrases and sentence patterns
  that always call for the subjunctive, memorized as patterns rather than derived from
  the abstract definition each time. *Antes de que* (before) is one of the simplest:
  it triggers the subjunctive automatically whenever the subject changes between the
  two clauses.
- Compare: *Suelo leer antes de acostarme* (I usually read before going to bed) — same
  subject in both parts, so it's just *antes de* + infinitive, no subjunctive needed
  at all.

## Examples

| Spanish                                              | English                                          | Notes                    |
| ------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------ |
| Ella escribe todos los días.                             | She writes every day.                                      | indicative, fact                |
| Espero que ella escriba pronto.                          | I hope she writes soon.                                     | subjunctive, hope/desire         |
| Sé que él trabaja aquí.                                  | I know he works here.                                       | indicative, certainty            |
| No creo que él trabaje aquí.                             | I don't think he works here.                                 | subjunctive, doubt               |
| Antes de que empiece la película, tengo que comprar palomitas. | Before the movie starts, I have to buy popcorn.        | subjunctive trigger, subject change |
| Suelo cepillarme los dientes antes de ir a la playa.     | I usually brush my teeth before going to the beach.          | no subject change, infinitive instead |

## Common Mistakes

- Trying to reason your way to the subjunctive from the abstract definition every
  time, instead of recognizing the trigger — this slows you down and leads to
  second-guessing sentences that don't feel "uncertain" but still need it (like
  *antes de que*).
- Assuming subjunctive only appears with obvious doubt words (*dudo que*) and missing
  it after desire, emotion, or fixed time/subordination triggers.
- Using *antes de que* + subjunctive even when there's no subject change — if the
  subject stays the same, drop *que* and use the infinitive instead: *antes de* +
  infinitive.
- Forgetting that the subjunctive lives in Spanish everyday speech, not just formal or
  literary contexts — it's not rare or optional the way English subjunctive can feel.

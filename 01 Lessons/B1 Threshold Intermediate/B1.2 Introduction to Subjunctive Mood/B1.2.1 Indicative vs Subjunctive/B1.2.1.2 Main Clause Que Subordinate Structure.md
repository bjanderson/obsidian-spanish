## Explanation

[[B1.2.1.1 Reality vs Subjectivity|The previous lesson]] introduced the concept of the
subjunctive mood. This lesson zooms into the actual sentence architecture: almost
every subjunctive sentence you'll build has the same three-part skeleton. Learn this
skeleton once, and you can plug in dozens of different triggers without relearning
the structure each time.

## Key Points / Conjugation / Rules

**The skeleton: main clause + que + subordinate clause (subjunctive):**

| Part                  | Role                                          | Example piece |
| ------------------------ | -------------------------------------------------- | ---------------- |
| Main clause               | states the trigger — desire, doubt, emotion, etc.    | *Quiero* |
| *que*                     | connects the two clauses                             | *que* |
| Subordinate clause         | contains the subjunctive verb                        | *vayas a la tienda* |

Put together: *Quiero que vayas a la tienda.* (I want you to go to the store.)

- The subject of the main clause and the subject of the subordinate clause must be
  **different people**. This is the single most important mechanical rule: *Quiero
  que vayas* (I want you to go — two different people, *yo* and *tú*) needs the
  subjunctive, but *Quiero ir* (I want to go — same subject, *yo* and *yo*) just uses
  the infinitive, no *que*, no subjunctive at all.
- You can swap in a different subject for the subordinate clause without changing the
  formula: *Quiero que Juan vaya a la tienda* (I want Juan to go to the store).
- The main clause's verb doesn't have to be about desire — it can be a time trigger
  (*cuando*), an emotion, a doubt, or an impersonal expression. Whatever it is, the
  mechanics stay the same: main clause, *que*, subordinate clause with the subjunctive
  verb.
- *Cuando* + subjunctive works the same way when the action hasn't happened yet:
  *Avísame cuando llegues a casa* (Let me know when you get home) — the arrival hasn't
  happened, so it's subjunctive. Compare this to *cuando* + indicative for habitual or
  completed actions, which you'll see doesn't use this trigger pattern.

## Examples

| Spanish                                              | English                                          | Notes                    |
| ------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------ |
| Quiero que vayas a la playa.                             | I want you to go to the beach.                             | main clause + *que* + subordinate |
| Quiero que Juan vaya a la tienda.                        | I want Juan to go to the store.                             | subject swapped in subordinate |
| Avísame cuando llegues a casa.                           | Let me know when you get home.                              | *cuando* trigger                |
| Avísame cuando tengas la información.                    | Let me know when you have the information.                   | *cuando* trigger                |
| Avísame cuando quieras ir a la playa.                    | Let me know when you want to go to the beach.                  | *cuando* trigger                |
| Quiero ir a la playa.                                    | I want to go to the beach.                                   | same subject → infinitive, no *que* |

## Common Mistakes

- Adding *que* + subjunctive even when the subject doesn't change: *Quiero que vaya a
  la playa* (intending "I want to go") is wrong if *yo* is doing both the wanting and
  the going — it should be *Quiero ir a la playa*.
- Forgetting *que* entirely and trying to jam the subordinate clause straight onto the
  main clause: *Quiero vayas a la tienda* is incomplete — *que* is required to connect
  the two clauses.
- Conjugating the subordinate verb in the indicative out of habit: *Quiero que vas* is
  wrong — once you're past *que* with a subject change, the subjunctive form is
  required: *vayas*.
- Assuming the main clause always needs an obvious "trigger word" like *quiero* — time
  expressions like *cuando* trigger it too, and the underlying two-clause mechanics
  are identical either way.

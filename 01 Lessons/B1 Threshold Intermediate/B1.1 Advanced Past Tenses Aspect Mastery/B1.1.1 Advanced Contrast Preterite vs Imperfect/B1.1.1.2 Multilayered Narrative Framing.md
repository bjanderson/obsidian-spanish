## Explanation

Real stories aren't just a single sentence in the preterite or the imperfect — they
layer both tenses together. The imperfect paints the background (what things were
like, what was already happening), and the preterite drops in the specific events
that moved the story forward. Learning to stack these layers is what makes your
Spanish storytelling sound natural instead of like a list of disconnected facts.

## Key Points / Conjugation / Rules

**The basic layering pattern:** imperfect sets the scene, preterite interrupts it
with an event.

- *Hacía frío y el cielo parecía que iba a nevar en cualquier momento.* (It was cold
  and the sky looked like it was going to snow at any moment.) — pure background, all
  imperfect.
- *Juan estaba en el ejército cuando conoció a María.* (Juan was in the Army when he
  met María.) — imperfect background + preterite event.

**Stacking multiple imperfect layers before the main event:**

A good story often piles up several imperfect clauses — describing the weather, the
mood, what people were doing — before the preterite verb finally moves the plot:

*Era de noche, llovía, y todos estaban cansados cuando por fin llegamos al hotel.* (It
was nighttime, it was raining, and everyone was tired when we finally arrived at the
hotel.)

**Preterite continuous vs. imperfect continuous** (*estar* + gerund):

| Form                  | Meaning                                          | Example |
| ------------------------ | ---------------------------------------------------- | --------- |
| Preterite continuous      | an action that lasted a defined stretch of time, now over | *Estuve leyendo dos horas.* (I was reading for two hours.) |
| Imperfect continuous      | an action in progress when something else happened     | *Estaba leyendo cuando me llamó María.* (I was reading when María called me.) |

**Using *deber* in the past to comment on a story:**

| Tense     | Meaning                                     | Example |
| ----------- | ----------------------------------------------- | --------- |
| Preterite   | should have (and didn't)                          | *Debí decirle la verdad.* (I should have told her the truth.) |
| Imperfect   | should (as advice given at the time)               | *Le dije a Juan que debía vender su casa.* (I told Juan he should sell his house.) |

- A common, very natural variant of the preterite *deber* meaning adds *haber* +
  past participle: *Debí haberle dicho la verdad* — same meaning, slightly more
  formal-sounding.
- These *deber* forms are a narrator's tool — they let you step back from the story
  and comment on what should have happened, which is a hallmark of natural,
  higher-level storytelling.

## Examples

| Spanish                                                    | English                                              | Notes                    |
| ---------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------ |
| Estábamos en el parque cuando empezó a llover.                   | We were in the park when it started to rain.                  | imperfect background + preterite event |
| Estaba leyendo un libro cuando sonó el teléfono.                  | I was reading a book when the phone rang.                     | imperfect continuous + preterite |
| Estuve trabajando toda la tarde en ese proyecto.                  | I was working on that project all afternoon.                  | preterite continuous            |
| Hacía calor, la calle estaba vacía, y de repente vi a mi vecino.  | It was hot, the street was empty, and suddenly I saw my neighbor. | stacked imperfect layers    |
| Debí haber estudiado más antes del examen.                       | I should have studied more before the exam.                    | *deber* + *haber*, preterite    |
| Le dije que debía llegar temprano, pero llegó tarde de todos modos. | I told him he should arrive early, but he arrived late anyway. | *deber*, imperfect + preterite  |

## Common Mistakes

- Writing an entire story in only one tense — a story told entirely in preterite
  sounds like a list of bullet points; entirely in imperfect, it never actually moves
  forward. Real narration needs both working together.
- Confusing preterite continuous and imperfect continuous: *Estuve leyendo cuando
  sonó el teléfono* sounds odd — the interrupted-background meaning calls for
  imperfect continuous: *Estaba leyendo cuando sonó el teléfono*.
- Using *debí* when you mean ongoing advice rather than regret: *debí* implies
  something wasn't done and you regret it now; for advice given in the moment
  (without that regret), the imperfect *debía* fits better.
- Overloading a sentence with too many stacked imperfect clauses before ever using
  the preterite — background needs a payoff event, or the story stalls.

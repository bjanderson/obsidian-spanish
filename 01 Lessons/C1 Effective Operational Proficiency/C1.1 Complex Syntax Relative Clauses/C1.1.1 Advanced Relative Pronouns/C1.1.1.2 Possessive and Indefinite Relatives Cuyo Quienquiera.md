## Explanation

Sometimes a relative clause needs to show that something belongs to the noun you
just mentioned — English does this with "whose." Spanish has a dedicated word for
that job: **cuyo**. Unlike English "whose," *cuyo* is not a question word here —
it works only inside a clause connecting two ideas, and it changes form to match
the *thing owned*, not the owner.

Spanish also has a small set of **indefinite relatives** — words like
*quienquiera* ("whoever") and *cualquiera* ("whichever/whoever/whatever") that
refer to a person or thing without specifying which one. They show up in more
formal or literary speech, often to sound sweeping or emphatic ("whoever it may
be," "no matter which one").

## Key Points / Conjugation / Rules

- **Cuyo agreement**: *cuyo, cuya, cuyos, cuyas* agree in gender and number with
  the noun that follows them (the thing possessed) — never with the possessor.
  - *El autor cuyo libro leímos* — "cuyo" agrees with *libro* (masc.), not *autor*.
  - *La mujer cuya casa visitamos* — "cuya" agrees with *casa* (fem.).
- *Cuyo* sits directly before the possessed noun, with no article in between:
  *cuya casa*, not *cuya la casa*.
- *Cuyo* is formal/literary. In everyday speech, people usually restructure the
  sentence with *de quien* or *que...de él/ella* instead.
- **Quienquiera** ("whoever") is invariable for gender but takes a plural form
  *quienesquiera* for multiple people. It's typically followed by *que*:
  *quienquiera que llame*.
- **Cualquiera** ("whichever/whatever/anyone") shortens to *cualquier* before a
  singular noun (*cualquier persona*), but keeps the full form when standing
  alone or after the noun (*una razón cualquiera*).
- Both indefinite relatives are common in set phrases and formal writing more
  than casual conversation — flag this if a lesson feels stiffer than usual.

## Examples

| Spanish | English | Notes |
| ------- | ------- | ----- |
| El escritor cuyo libro ganó el premio dio una entrevista. | The writer whose book won the prize gave an interview. | *Cuyo* agrees with *libro* (masc.), the thing possessed. |
| La empresa cuyos empleados renunciaron tuvo que cerrar. | The company whose employees quit had to close. | *Cuyos* agrees with *empleados* (masc. pl.). |
| Conocí a la artista cuya pintura está en el museo. | I met the artist whose painting is in the museum. | *Cuya* agrees with *pintura* (fem.). |
| Quienquiera que gane el concurso recibirá un premio. | Whoever wins the contest will receive a prize. | *Quienquiera que* + subjunctive for an unknown person. |
| Cualquiera que sea tu decisión, te apoyamos. | Whatever your decision may be, we support you. | *Cualquiera que* + subjunctive for an unspecified option. |
| Puedes llamar a cualquier persona del equipo. | You can call any person on the team. | *Cualquier* shortens before the noun. |
| Los vecinos cuyas quejas llegaron a la alcaldía fueron escuchados. | The neighbors whose complaints reached city hall were heard. | *Cuyas* agrees with *quejas* (fem. pl.). |

## Common Mistakes

- Making *cuyo* agree with the possessor instead of the thing possessed:
  ~~la mujer cuyo casa~~ → **la mujer cuya casa** (agrees with *casa*, feminine).
- Inserting an article after *cuyo*: ~~cuyo el libro~~ → **cuyo libro**.
- Using *cuyo* as a question word ("whose is this?") — Spanish uses *¿de quién?*
  for questions, never *cuyo*.
- Using the full form *cualquiera* directly before a singular noun instead of
  the shortened *cualquier*: ~~cualquiera persona~~ → **cualquier persona**.

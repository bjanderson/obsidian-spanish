## Explanation

A gerund on its own (*hablando, comiendo, viviendo*) can start a clause that
explains **how, why, or under what circumstance** the main action happens —
this is called an **adverbial gerund clause**. It works like a compressed
"while/since/by doing X" clause, with no subject and no conjugation needed,
placed at the front or the end of the sentence.

At the C1 level, the key addition is combining the gerund of *haber* —
**habiendo** — with a past participle to form the **compound gerund**
(*habiendo hablado, habiendo terminado*). This version points to something
that happened **before** the main action, similar in spirit to the absolute
participle clause, but phrased as "having done X" instead of "once X was
done."

## Key Points / Conjugation / Rules

- Simple gerund clause = *[gerund], [main clause]*. Explains manner, cause, or
  simultaneous action: *Trabajando duro, logró su meta.* (By working hard, she
  achieved her goal.)
- Compound gerund clause = *habiendo + past participle*, for an action
  completed **before** the main clause: *Habiendo terminado el informe, se
  fue a casa.* (Having finished the report, she went home.)
- The past participle inside *habiendo + participle* **never changes form** —
  unlike the absolute participle construction, this participle is part of a
  verb form, not acting as an adjective: *habiendo terminado*, always, no
  matter the gender/number of the subject.
- The gerund clause's implied subject must match the main clause's subject —
  if the subjects are different, this construction doesn't work and you need
  a full clause with *como*, *ya que*, or *después de que* instead.
- This is a formal/literary construction — very common in essays, reports,
  and news writing, rare in everyday spoken Spanish.

## Examples

| Spanish | English | Notes |
| ------- | ------- | ----- |
| Habiendo considerado todos los factores, decidimos aceptar la oferta. | Having considered all the factors, we decided to accept the offer. | Compound gerund, action before the main clause. |
| Trabajando juntos, terminaron el proyecto antes de tiempo. | By working together, they finished the project ahead of schedule. | Simple gerund clause, expresses manner. |
| Habiendo leído el contrato completo, firmé sin dudar. | Having read the whole contract, I signed without hesitation. | Compound gerund, same implied subject as main clause. |
| Sabiendo que llovería, llevamos paraguas. | Knowing it would rain, we brought umbrellas. | Simple gerund clause, expresses cause/reason. |
| Habiendo resuelto el conflicto, el equipo pudo enfocarse en el trabajo. | Having resolved the conflict, the team could focus on the work. | *Resuelto* stays unchanged — part of the verb form, not an adjective. |
| Viviendo tan lejos, casi nunca los vemos. | Living so far away, we almost never see them. | Simple gerund clause, expresses circumstance. |

## Common Mistakes

- Changing the participle's ending inside *habiendo + participle*, as if it
  were agreeing like an adjective: ~~habiendo terminada~~ → **habiendo
  terminado** (always masculine singular form here).
- Using this construction when the subjects of the two clauses differ — that
  needs a full conjugated clause instead: ~~Habiendo llegado tarde, el jefe se
  enojó~~ (unclear who arrived late) → **Como llegamos tarde, el jefe se
  enojó.**
- Overusing gerund clauses in casual conversation, where a plain conjugated
  clause with *cuando*, *porque*, or *como* sounds far more natural.
- Confusing the simple gerund clause (simultaneous/manner) with the compound
  gerund clause (prior completed action) — *hablando* ≠ *habiendo hablado*.

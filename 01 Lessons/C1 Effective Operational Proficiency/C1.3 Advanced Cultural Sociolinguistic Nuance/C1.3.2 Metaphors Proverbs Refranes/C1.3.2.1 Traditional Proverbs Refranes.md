## Explanation

Every language has a stock of short, fixed sayings that carry a whole piece of folk
wisdom in one memorable line — the kind of thing a grandparent says instead of giving
a long lecture. In Spanish these are called **refranes** (proverbs, sayings), and
native speakers reach for them constantly in everyday conversation: to give advice, to
comment on a situation, to comfort someone, or just to sound wise and a little funny at
the same time.

The tricky part for a learner isn't the vocabulary — it's that a refrán almost never
means what its words say literally. *Más vale pajarito en mano que cien volando*
literally talks about a little bird in your hand versus a hundred flying around, but
no one who says it is talking about birds. They're telling you to take the sure, smaller
thing in front of you instead of gambling on something bigger and uncertain. Learning
refranes is really learning to recognize a fixed shape of words and instantly jump to
its figurative meaning, the same way an English speaker doesn't picture an actual bird
when they hear "a bird in the hand."

The other thing that makes refranes distinct from ordinary sentences is that their
wording is **fixed** — almost like a quotation everyone already knows. You don't
conjugate them differently, swap words for synonyms, or rephrase them for your own
subject; you say the whole thing exactly as it's always said, the way you'd quote a
familiar line rather than build a new sentence. Drop one into a conversation at the
right moment and it lands as a shared cultural reference, not just as information.

## Key Points / Conjugation / Rules

- ***Más vale pajarito en mano que cien volando*** — literally "a little bird in the
  hand is worth more than a hundred flying," figuratively "a bird in the hand is worth
  two in the bush." Said when someone is about to give up a modest sure thing while
  chasing a bigger, riskier prize. **Regional variant:** many speakers shorten it to
  *más vale pájaro en mano que cien volando* (no diminutive *-ito*) — both are widely
  understood and used interchangeably.
- ***Camarón que se duerme, se lo lleva la corriente*** — literally "the shrimp that
  falls asleep gets carried away by the current," figuratively "you snooze, you lose."
  Said about someone who missed an opportunity, a deal, or a deadline by being too slow
  or too passive to act.
- ***A caballo regalado no se le mira el diente*** — literally "you don't check a
  gifted horse's teeth," figuratively "don't look a gift horse in the mouth." Said when
  someone starts criticizing or nitpicking something they received for free.
- ***Más vale tarde que nunca*** — literally and figuratively "better late than
  never." Said to reassure someone (or oneself) that finally doing something is still
  worthwhile, even after a long delay.
- ***El que mucho abarca poco aprieta*** — literally "he who grasps too much holds on
  to little," figuratively close to "jack of all trades, master of none" or "don't
  spread yourself too thin." Said about someone taking on too many projects or
  commitments at once and doing none of them well.
- ***No hay mal que por bien no venga*** — literally "there's no bad from which good
  doesn't come," figuratively "every cloud has a silver lining." Said to find the
  hidden upside in a setback or piece of bad news.
- ***Al mal tiempo, buena cara*** — literally "to bad weather, a good face,"
  figuratively "keep a positive attitude in a hard situation" (close to "chin up" or
  "make the best of it"). Said as encouragement when things are going badly and there's
  no way to fix them immediately.
- ***Del dicho al hecho hay mucho trecho*** — literally "from the saying to the deed
  there's a long stretch," figuratively "easier said than done." Said when someone's
  plans or promises sound great but actually pulling them off is another matter
  entirely.
- ***En boca cerrada no entran moscas*** — literally "flies don't get into a closed
  mouth," figuratively "silence is golden" or "better to keep your mouth shut." Said as
  advice (or a gentle warning) to someone who's about to say something better left
  unsaid.
- ***Cría fama y échate a dormir*** — literally "build a reputation and go lie down,"
  figuratively "once you've got a reputation, it sticks — for better or worse." Said
  about how hard it is to shake off a reputation, good or bad, once it's established.

## Common Mistakes

- **Taking a refrán literally instead of figuratively** — hearing *a caballo regalado
  no se le mira el diente* and thinking it's actual advice about horses, instead of
  recognizing it as a stock line about not criticizing free gifts. A refrán's surface
  meaning is almost never the point.
- **Misquoting the fixed wording** — these phrases don't flex like ordinary sentences.
  Saying *más vale un pájaro en la mano que ciento volando* with extra articles, or
  swapping *camarón* for *gamba* mid-phrase, breaks the recognizable shape and makes it
  sound like a mistake rather than a saying. Learn each refrán as one fixed unit, not as
  a sentence you can freely rebuild.
- **Using a refrán in the wrong emotional register** — dropping *al mal tiempo, buena
  cara* on someone who's genuinely grieving (rather than facing a minor inconvenience)
  can land as dismissive instead of encouraging. Match the weight of the refrán to the
  weight of the situation.
- **Forcing a refrán into a conversation where it doesn't fit** — refranes work as a
  natural capstone to a point someone's already making, not as a random inserted fact.
  Using one out of nowhere, disconnected from what's being discussed, reads as
  unnatural rather than wise.

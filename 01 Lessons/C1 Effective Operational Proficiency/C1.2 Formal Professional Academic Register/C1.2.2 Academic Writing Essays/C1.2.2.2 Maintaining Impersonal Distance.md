## Explanation

Think about how a scientific report, a news article, or a policy paper reads
compared to a text message. A report doesn't say "I think the results are
important" — it says something closer to "the results are considered
important." It doesn't say "someone published the study in March" — it says
"the study was published in March." The writer's own voice steps back, and the
facts take center stage. That stepping-back is what this lesson is about:
**objective, impersonal register** — the set of grammatical tools Spanish uses
to describe facts, findings, and events without pointing to who is doing the
describing.

Spanish leans on three main tools for this. The first is the **impersonal
*se*** (*se cree que..., se estima que...*), which lets you state a general
fact or belief without naming who holds it — the Spanish equivalent of
English "it is believed that..." or the vague "they say...". The second is
the **passive *se*** (*se publicaron los resultados*), which shifts the focus
onto what happened to something rather than who did it — very close in
meaning to English passive voice, but built with *se* + a verb that agrees
with the noun, not with *ser*. The third is the **passive voice with *ser***
(*fue aprobado por el comité*), which Spanish does have and does use, mostly
in written, formal register when the agent matters enough to mention.

On top of these three grammatical constructions, objective writing also means
making choices about *what* you write, not just *how*. That means swapping
first-person opinion phrases (*yo creo que, en mi opinión*) for impersonal
framing (*cabe señalar que, se puede argumentar que, los datos sugieren
que*), and swapping subjective, emotionally loaded adjectives (*un resultado
increíble, una política terrible*) for neutral, evidence-based language (*un
resultado significativo, una política controvertida*). None of this is about
being cold or robotic — it's about letting the facts carry the weight instead
of the writer's personal stance, which is exactly what academic and
professional readers expect.

## Key Points / Conjugation / Rules

**Impersonal *se*** — states a general fact, belief, or practice with no
specific subject

- Formula: *se* + verb in the **third person singular**, regardless of what
  follows.
- *Se cree que el clima está cambiando más rápido de lo previsto.* (It is
  believed that...)
- *Se estima que el proyecto costará dos millones de dólares.* (It is
  estimated that...)
- Works with intransitive verbs and verbs followed by a clause (*que...*) —
  there's no direct object for the verb to agree with, so it always stays
  singular: *se vive bien aquí*, *se trabaja mucho en esa empresa*.

**Passive *se*** — shifts focus onto what happened to something, without
naming who did it

- Formula: *se* + verb that **agrees in number** with the noun affected by
  the action (the grammatical subject of the sentence).
- *Se publicaron los resultados del estudio en abril.* (The results were
  published...) — *resultados* is plural, so the verb is plural.
- *Se aprobó la nueva ley la semana pasada.* (The new law was approved...) —
  *ley* is singular, so the verb is singular.
- Only works with transitive verbs (verbs that normally take a direct
  object) — that direct object becomes the sentence's grammatical subject
  and controls verb agreement.

**Passive voice with *ser*** — formal, written register; useful when the
agent matters

- Formula: *ser* (conjugated) + past participle (agrees in gender/number
  with the subject) + optional *por* + agent.
- *La ley fue aprobada por el Congreso en marzo.* (The law was approved by
  Congress in March.)
- *Los resultados fueron confirmados por un segundo estudio.* (The results
  were confirmed by a second study.)
- More common in written/formal Spanish than in speech, and more natural
  than passive *se* specifically when you need to state *who* performed the
  action.

**Avoiding first person** — impersonal phrasing instead of stating an
opinion directly

- Instead of *yo creo que* / *pienso que* → *cabe señalar que, se puede
  argumentar que, los datos indican que, cabe destacar que, existe consenso
  en que*.
- Instead of *en mi opinión* → *desde una perspectiva objetiva, según los
  datos disponibles, de acuerdo con el estudio*.
- The goal is to attribute the claim to evidence or general consensus, not
  to the writer's personal stance.

**Avoiding subjective adjectives** — neutral, evidence-based word choice

- Swap emotionally loaded adjectives (*increíble, terrible, maravilloso,
  espantoso*) for neutral, measurable ones (*significativo, considerable,
  controvertido, limitado*).
- Back up a claim with a number, a source, or a comparison instead of an
  adjective alone: not *un aumento enorme*, but *un aumento del quince por
  ciento*.

- All three grammatical tools (impersonal *se*, passive *se*, *ser*-passive)
  can appear in the same paragraph — good academic writing mixes them rather
  than repeating one construction throughout.

## Examples

| Spanish | English | Notes |
| ------- | ------- | ----- |
| **Se considera** que el calentamiento global es la mayor amenaza ambiental de este siglo. | It is considered that global warming is the greatest environmental threat of this century. | Impersonal *se* + third-person singular *considera*, no matter what follows. |
| **Se publicaron** los datos preliminares del censo la semana pasada. | The preliminary census data were published last week. | Passive *se*; *datos* is plural, so *publicaron* agrees in the plural. |
| La reforma constitucional **fue aprobada por** una mayoría de dos tercios. | The constitutional reform was approved by a two-thirds majority. | *Ser*-passive names the agent (*una mayoría de dos tercios*) explicitly with *por*. |
| **Cabe señalar que** el quince por ciento de los participantes abandonó el estudio antes de su finalización. | It is worth noting that fifteen percent of the participants dropped out of the study before its completion. | Impersonal phrase replaces a first-person opener like *yo noto que*. |
| El informe concluye que la política tuvo un efecto **limitado** sobre el desempleo juvenil. | The report concludes that the policy had a limited effect on youth unemployment. | Neutral adjective (*limitado*) instead of a subjective one like *decepcionante*. |
| **Se estima que** el mercado crecerá un ocho por ciento durante el próximo año fiscal. | It is estimated that the market will grow eight percent during the next fiscal year. | Impersonal *se* is standard for forecasts and projections in economic reports. |
| Los primeros manuscritos de la región **fueron hallados** por un equipo de arqueólogos en 1987. | The first manuscripts from the region were found by a team of archaeologists in 1987. | *Ser*-passive fits historical/archival claims where crediting the discoverer matters. |

## Common Mistakes

- **Confusing impersonal *se* with passive *se* and getting agreement
  wrong.** Impersonal *se* always stays singular (*se necesita más
  información*, not *se necesitan más información*, unless *información* is
  genuinely functioning as the grammatical subject of a passive construction).
  When in doubt, ask whether there's a plural noun that the verb should agree
  with — if yes, it's passive *se*; if there's no clear noun-subject (just a
  general statement), it's impersonal *se* and stays singular.
- **Slipping into first person mid-text.** A paragraph that opens with *se
  argumenta que...* and then continues with *yo pienso que esto es
  correcto* breaks the objective register the writer just established.
  Academic and report writing should stay in impersonal phrasing throughout,
  not just in the topic sentence.
- **Reaching for *ser* + past participle when *se* passive is more natural.**
  Spanish uses the *se* passive far more often than English uses its passive
  voice. *Los boletos se venden en la entrada* sounds natural; *los boletos
  son vendidos en la entrada* sounds like a stiff, over-literal translation
  from English. Save the *ser*-passive for formal written contexts where the
  agent needs stating.
- **Over-literal translation of English passive constructions.** English
  passive sentences without a clear agent (*It was decided that...*, *Mistakes
  were made*) don't automatically map onto *ser* + participle in Spanish —
  *se decidió que...* is the natural equivalent, not *fue decidido que...*.
- **Forgetting the past participle needs gender/number agreement in the
  *ser*-passive.** *La ley fue aprobada* (feminine singular), not *la ley fue
  aprobado* — the participle behaves like an adjective here, unlike in the
  compound tenses with *haber*.

# **I am looking at a complex sentence.**

**"What is a complex sentence?** 

**A complex sentence communicates more than one idea.**

- One part (the main clause) is like a simple sentence: it can stand on its own: [subject + verb = a complete thought].
- The other part (the [dependent] clause) gives more detail about the simple sentence, but it cannot stand alone [even though it too has a subject + verb]."

**"[L]ook at this sentence: [I always go out to greet my dad when he arrives.]**

- The main clause is **[I always go out to greet my dad].** It has a subject (I) and a verb (go out) and makes sense by itself.
- The [dependent] clause is [**'When he arrived'**]. Although it is a clause with a subject (he) and a verb (arrives), it doesn't make sense on its own. [We have to glue the connector (when) to the main clause for it to make sense. In Spanish, the connector may cause the verb in the dependent clause to change its "mood" from indicative to subjunctive.]"

# **RULES:** (**Relative Pronouns**) SOURCES: Vistas, p. <sup>415</sup>

- 1. **Que:** "**Que** is the most frequently used relative pronoun. It can refer to things or people. Unlike English, "that", "**que" is never omitted in Spanish**."
- 2. **Quien**: "The relative pronoun **quien** refers only to people, and is often used after a preposition or the personal **a.** It has two forms: quien and quienes."
- 3. **Lo que: "Unlike que and quien, lo que** refers to an idea, a situation, or a past event and means *what, that which*, or *the thing that*."

"Let's break it down step by step.

- 1. \*\*Objeto indirecto (Indirect object):\*\* In the sentence 16 "Me preocupa que mi hijo esté enfermo," the indirect object is \*\*"me"\*\*. It shows who is affected by the action of the verb "preocupa" (who is worried). In this case, "me" refers to "I" (the person feeling worried).
- 2. \*\*Cláusula de nombre (Noun clause):\*\* The noun clause is \*\*"que mi hijo esté enfermo."\*\* It acts as the subject of the verb "preocupa." This clause answers the question \*what worries me?\* (The fact that my son is sick worries me).
- 3. \*\*Traducción al inglés (Translation):\*\* The sentence translates to: \*\*"It worries me that my son is sick."\*\* Notice how the noun clause in Spanish is introduced by "que," and in English, it becomes "that."

In Spanish, the verb \*\*"preocupar"\*\* is a \*\*backward verb\*\* (like "gustar"). This means the subject of the sentence is the thing causing the worry, while the person who feels worried is indicated by an indirect object pronoun (like "me," "te," "le," etc.). - \*\*Me preocupa que mi hijo esté enfermo.\*\* Translation: \*"It worries me that my son is sick."\*

In English, [although] we usually say "I am worried" instead of "It worries me." the focus [in English] shifts to \*your emotional state\*, rather than the cause of it [as it would in Spanish]. English places the subject-noun-clause at the end of the sentence for clarity and flow. Both are grammatically correct [in English], but are slightly different in structure and emphasis in Spanish.

In simpler terms, verbs like \*\*"sorprender,"\*\* \*\*"molestar,"\*\* and \*\*"preocupar"\*\* naturally emphasize the external cause of the emotion, not your internal state. When translating into English, we sometimes adapt them to sound more natural by saying "I am worried," "I am surprised" or "I am bothered," but the structure and nuance in Spanish are not identical."

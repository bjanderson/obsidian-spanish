# **Qroo Spanish Crew Lesson Summary / Would**

English speakers use *would* all the time — to sound polite, to describe habits, to talk about hypotheticals, or to express things that *would have* happened.

But in Spanish, *would* doesn't always translate to the conditional tense.

In this lesson, we break down how to express "would" and "would have" naturally, without falling into the direct-translation trap.

# **1. "I Would Like" — Ordering and Expressing Desire Naturally**

When English speakers first learn the conditional, *me gustaría* (I would like) is usually one of the first phrases they memorize — but in practice, it's **not how most natives order food or drinks**.

You'll rarely hear someone say:

*Me gustaría una hamburguesa.*

That sounds overly formal or touristy. Native speakers usually choose from other much more natural options depending on tone and region:

*Quiero una hamburguesa.* – I want a hamburger. *Quisiera una hamburguesa.* – I'd like a hamburger. (a bit softer, very polite) *¿Podría tener un café?* / *¿Podría traerme un café?* – Could I have / Could you bring me a coffee?

In **Spain**, you'll also hear:

¿Me pones un café? – Literally "You put to me a coffee."

That's their common way to order — completely natural there.

# **Note:**

This list of ways to order is not complete. How people order varies by country and even by region. For example, in **Colombia**, you'll often hear *regalar* (literally "to give as a gift") used politely when ordering:

*¿Me regalas un tinto, por favor?* – Could you give me a coffee, please?

#### **When is "me gustaría" okay?**

My advice is to stick to using me gustaría with other verbs. For example:

*Me gustaría viajar a México.* – I'd like to travel to Mexico.

*Me gustaría salir a cenar.* – I'd like to go out to dinner.

#### **Pattern to remember:**

*Me gustaría + infinitive* → to express polite desire, not for ordering.

#### **2. Polite Requests — Present Tense Is Usually Enough**

In English, *would* makes things sound polite:

*Would you help me? Would you pass the salt?*

In Spanish, the plain old present tense is often used to make polite requests.

Examples:

*¿Me ayudas, por favor?* – Would you help me, please? *¿Me pasas la sal?* – Would you pass me the salt? *¿Me traes un vaso de agua?* – Would you bring me a glass of water?

It may sound direct in English, but in Spanish it's perfectly natural — your tone conveys politeness.

# **3. "Would You Mind…?" —** *¿Te importaría...?* **/** *¿Le importaría...?*

Here's one case where the **conditional** actually is used in Spanish.

### **Structure:**

*¿Te importaría + infinitive?* – informal (tú form)

*¿Le importaría + infinitive?* – formal (usted form)

Examples:

*¿Te importaría ayudarme?* – Would you mind helping me?

*¿Le importaría cerrar la puerta?* – Would you mind closing the door?

#### **Formula:**

*(Te/Le) importaría + infinitive*

It's a polite, natural way to make requests and one that's worth memorizing.

#### **4. Hypotheticals —"If I Didn't Have to Work, I Would Go…"**

This is where *would* really matches the **conditional** tense.

We use *si* (if) clauses that combine the **imperfect subjunctive** and the **conditional**.

### **Formula:**

*Si + imperfect subjunctive → conditional tense*

Example:

*Si yo no tuviera que trabajar, iría a la playa contigo.*

(If I didn't have to work, I would go to the beach with you.)

Breakdown:

- *Si no tuviera que trabajar* → imperfect subjunctive
- *iría a la playa contigo* → conditional

# **5. "Would" for Repeated or Habitual Actions in the Past**

In English, *would* can mean *used to*:

"When I was a kid, I would play outside every day."

In Spanish, that's **not** the conditional — it's the **imperfect tense**.

Example:

*Cuando era niño, jugaba afuera todos los días.*

(When I was a kid, I would play outside every day.)

So, when *would* means *used to*, always use the **imperfect**.

# **6. Talking About the Future from the Past**

When you use *would* to talk about something in the future relative to a past moment, Spanish mirrors English perfectly — and this time the conditional works.

#### Examples:

*Dijo que llegaría tarde.* – He said he would arrive late.

*Pensé que me llamarías.* – I thought you would call me.

This structure feels natural for English speakers because the logic is the same in both languages.

# **7. "Would Have…" — The Conditional Perfect**

For *would have*, we use **haber** in the conditional plus a **past participle**.

#### **Formula:**

*haber (conditional) + participle*

Examples:

*Habría ido, pero estaba enfermo.* – I would have gone, but I was sick.

*Te habría llamado, pero no tenía tu número.* – I would have called you, but I didn't have your number.

It's close to English and follows the same logic once you recognize *haber* as the helper verb.

# **8. Hypotheticals with "Would Have"**

Combine two clauses — one with *si* and one with *would have.*

#### **Formula:**

*Si + past perfect (pluperfect) subjunctive → conditional perfect*

Examples:

*Si yo hubiera sabido la verdad, no habría ido a la fiesta.*

(If I had known the truth, I wouldn't have gone to the party.)

*Habrían venido si hubieran tenido más tiempo.*

(They would have come if they had had more time.)

# **Shortcut:**

In real speech, many native speakers use the **past perfect subjunctive** in both clauses:

That's very common and perfectly natural — though *hubiera... habría...* is the more standard, textbook form.

#### **1. Tastes Like –** *Saber a*

- *Saber* can mean "to taste" (when referring to how something tastes, not the act of tasting).
- To say something tastes like something else, use **saber a + noun**. <sup>o</sup> *La sopa sabe a pescado.* – The soup tastes like fish. <sup>o</sup> *¿A qué sabe el conejo?* – What does rabbit taste like? <sup>o</sup> *Sabe a pollo.* – It tastes like chicken.
- To say "taste this," use *probar* (not *saber*): <sup>o</sup> *¿Quieres probar mi café?* – Do you want to try my coffee?

## **2. Smells Like –** *Oler a*

- *Oler* means "to smell" (either emitting a smell or perceiving it).
- To say something smells like something, use **oler a + noun**. <sup>o</sup> *La comida huele a pescado.* – The food smells like fish. <sup>o</sup> *Estos zapatos huelen a pies.* – These shoes smell like feet.
- In Latin America, you might hear *huele feo* ("smells bad") instead of *huele mal*.

## **3. Sounds Like –** *Sonar a* **and** *Sonar como*

- Use **sonar a + noun** for abstract or figurative comparisons: <sup>o</sup> *Suena a mentira.* – It sounds like a lie.
- Use **sonar como + noun** for literal comparisons:

- <sup>o</sup> *Los fuegos artificiales suenan como disparos.* The fireworks sound like gunshots.
- For more advanced learners: <sup>o</sup> *Suena como si + imperfect subjunctive* means "It sounds as if…" ▪*Suena como si supieras la verdad.* – It sounds as if you knew the truth.

#### **4. Looks Like –** *Parecerse a***,** *Parecer***,** *Verse*

- **Parecerse a** is used to compare physical appearance (requires *a*): <sup>o</sup> *Se parece a tu primo.* – He looks like your cousin. <sup>o</sup> *Nos parecemos.* – We look alike.
- **Parecer** is used for general impressions or ideas (can mean "seems like"): <sup>o</sup> *Parece actriz.* – She looks like an actress. <sup>o</sup> *Parece que va a llover.* – It looks like it's going to rain.
- **Verse** is used for visual impressions or appearance: <sup>o</sup> *Te ves bien.* – You look good. <sup>o</sup> *La casa se ve bonita.* – The house looks pretty.

### **Conclusion:**

Sensory expressions in Spanish don't always translate word-for-word from English, especially when prepositions are involved. Learning how to say "tastes like," "smells like," "sounds like," and "looks like" requires using the correct verbs—*saber a*, *oler a*, *sonar a/comó*, *parecer*, *parecerse a*, and *verse*—along with the right prepositions and conjugations. By mastering these patterns and formulas, you can describe sensory experiences naturally and accurately in Spanish. As always, the key is to recognize the pattern, plug in the vocabulary, and practice forming real sentences.
